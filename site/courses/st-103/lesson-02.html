There is a temptation, when studying the Holy Spirit, to treat the Old Testament as little more than a waiting room. We rush through it looking for Pentecost, as though nothing of substance happened with the Spirit until the New Testament arrived.

That instinct misses something important. The Spirit of God was never absent from the story of Israel. He was active from the very first verse of Genesis, and tracing His work through the Old Testament gives us a richer understanding of what changed, and what did not, when the New Covenant began.

This lesson is the hinge of the whole course.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Describe the Spirit's activity in creation and in the empowerment of individuals for specific tasks.
  2. Explain the pattern of the Spirit coming upon people, and the significance of the Spirit departing from Saul.
  3. Explain the Spirit's role in prophetic speech.
  4. Identify the Old Testament promises of a greater outpouring in Moses, Ezekiel, Jeremiah, and Joel.
  5. State precisely what changed at Pentecost and what did not.
  6. Explain why the indwelling Spirit is the birthright of every believer rather than a second blessing.

1. Hovering Over the Deep

Genesis opens with the earth formless and void, darkness over the face of the deep, and then, in the same breath, the Spirit of God hovering over the face of the waters. The Hebrew word behind hovering pictures a bird suspended over its nest, wings outstretched, protective and attentive.

Before light existed, before land separated from sea, before a single living thing drew breath, the Spirit of God was present, active, and engaged in the work of creation.

This establishes something foundational. The Spirit's work did not begin with humanity's need for salvation. It began with God's own creative purpose, present from eternity, involved in bringing order out of chaos and life out of nothing. Job would later testify that the Spirit of God had made him and the breath of the Almighty had given him life.

2. Empowering for Specific Tasks

As the story of Israel unfolds, we see the Spirit coming upon particular individuals for particular purposes, almost always tied to a specific calling rather than a permanent indwelling.

When God commissioned Bezalel to oversee the construction of the tabernacle, He filled him with the Spirit of God, giving him wisdom, understanding, and knowledge in all kinds of craftsmanship, from working gold and silver to cutting stones and carving wood. This was not a vague spiritual sensation. It was practical, skilled competence given for a specific project.

Where the Spirit works

This is a reminder that the Spirit's work is never confined to what we consider strictly religious activity. He equips people to build, to create, to labour with excellence, because all of it can be an offering to God.

The judges present a different pattern. When the Spirit of the Lord came upon Gideon, Scripture says the Spirit clothed him, an image of empowerment wrapped around a man who considered himself the least in his family and his family the weakest in his tribe. The same Spirit came upon Samson repeatedly, granting bursts of supernatural strength tied to specific moments of deliverance.

In each case the emphasis falls on task and moment rather than ongoing relationship.

3. The Sobering Case of Saul

This becomes clearer, and more sobering, with Israel's first king. The Spirit of the Lord came upon Saul, and he prophesied among the prophets, evidence of genuine if temporary empowerment. But after Saul's persistent disobedience we read one of the most unsettling lines in the Old Testament: the Spirit of the Lord departed from Saul, and a distressing spirit troubled him instead.

Under the Old Covenant, the Spirit's presence with an individual could be withdrawn.

This is precisely why David, after his sin with Bathsheba, pleaded with God not to take His Holy Spirit from him. David had watched it happen to Saul. He knew it was possible. He was not being dramatic. He was being honest about the terms of the covenant he lived under.
Chapter Three

Feel the weight of that contrast, because it sets up everything that changes in the New Covenant. Under the Old Covenant, even a king anointed by God could lose the Spirit's presence through persistent rebellion.

4. A Voice in the Prophets

The Spirit's Old Testament work was not limited to physical strength or practical skill. He was the source of prophetic speech itself. Peter would later explain that prophecy never had its origin in the will of man, but that men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit. Ezekiel describes the Spirit entering him and setting him on his feet before God speaks through him.

The prophets did not invent their message. They received it, carried along by a Spirit who superintended their words even while using their individual personalities, vocabularies, and circumstances.

This is why Old Testament prophecy retains such coherence across centuries and multiple authors who never met one another. The consistent thread running through Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the minor prophets is not a shared human agenda. It is a shared divine Author.

5. Promises of Something More

Even within the Old Testament there are unmistakable signs that the Spirit's work as Israel then knew it was not the final word.

Moses expressed a longing that reaches forward into the New Covenant, wishing that all the Lord's people were prophets, that the Lord would put His Spirit upon all of them. That wish, spoken in the wilderness, would not be fulfilled for well over a thousand years.

Ezekiel, writing to a nation in exile, delivered the clearest Old Testament picture of what the New Covenant would accomplish: a new heart, a new spirit, the heart of stone removed, and God's own Spirit placed within His people, causing them to walk in His statutes.

Jeremiah promised a covenant different in kind rather than merely in degree. God would put His law within them and write it on their hearts, so that they would know Him from the least to the greatest.

Notice what this promise does not say. It does not promise better external rules, clearer instructions, or a more diligent effort at obedience. It promises an internal transformation so thorough that the law itself would no longer feel like an external demand but an internal reality.
Chapter Four

Joel painted the broadest picture: God pouring out His Spirit on all flesh, not only on kings, priests, and prophets but on sons and daughters, old men and young men, servants and free alike. The exclusivity that characterized the Spirit's Old Testament ministry was about to end.

6. Streams of Living Water

Jesus stood in the temple during the Feast of Tabernacles and announced that if anyone thirsts, let him come and drink, and that out of the heart of the one who believes will flow rivers of living water. John, writing decades later, explains exactly what Jesus meant. He was speaking about the Spirit, whom those who believed in Him would receive, for the Spirit had not yet been given because Jesus was not yet glorified.

That final clause deserves careful attention. The Spirit's coming in this new, indwelling capacity was tied directly to the completion of Christ's work.

Not a second blessing

The indwelling Spirit is not a gift given independently of the cross but a gift secured by it. The New Testament does not describe two classes of Christians, those who have Christ and those who additionally have the Spirit. Paul states the matter plainly: if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, he does not belong to Christ. The Spirit's indwelling is not an upgrade available to especially devoted believers. It is the birthright of every believer.

7. The Day Everything Changed

Ten days after Jesus ascended, on the Jewish feast of Pentecost, the promise arrived. The disciples were gathered when a sound like a rushing mighty wind filled the house, and divided tongues, as of fire, rested on each of them. They were all filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in other tongues as the Spirit gave them utterance.

The crowd that gathered represented the known world, and each heard the wonders of God declared in their own native language. This detail matters beyond its immediate drama. Babel, generations earlier, had scattered humanity through confused languages as a judgment on human pride. Pentecost began to reverse that scattering, gathering the nations back together through a shared understanding of God's mighty works. What sin had fractured, the Spirit began to reunite.

Peter stood and reached back to Joel to explain it. This, he declared, is what was spoken through the prophet Joel. Then he extended the offer that remains open to every person who hears the gospel since: repent and be baptized, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit, for the promise is for you and for your children and for all who are far off.

Ordinary people

The people filled with the Spirit that day were, for the most part, entirely ordinary. Fishermen, a former tax collector, women who had followed Jesus without any recorded title or office. There was no elite tier of especially qualified candidates. You do not need an impressive spiritual resume to receive the fullness of what the New Covenant offers.

8. Sons and Daughters, Not Servants

Paul's letter to the Galatians ties the story together. Because we are sons, God has sent the Spirit of His Son into our hearts, crying out, Abba, Father.

This is not the language of a servant awaiting instructions from a distant master. It is the language of family. The Spirit does not come to remind us of our failures or press us toward earning what we already possess. He comes to confirm, again and again, that we belong to the Father, adopted, secure, and loved.

9. What Changed, and What Did Not

This is the section to master.

Under the Old Covenant Under the New Covenant
The Spirit came upon select individuals The Spirit dwells within every believer
Empowerment was tied to task and moment Presence is permanent and continuous
Presence could be withdrawn, as with Saul Presence is sealed until the day of redemption
Reserved for prophets, priests, kings, and judges Poured out on all flesh, without distinction
What changed at Pentecost was not the Spirit's character. It was the terms of His dwelling.
Chapter Three

Understanding this history protects us from two errors. It keeps us from dismissing the Old Testament as spiritually irrelevant, since the same Spirit who indwells believers today was actively at work throughout its pages. And it keeps us from importing Old Testament patterns wholesale into New Covenant experience, assuming the Spirit's presence is as conditional and selective for us as it was for Saul.

One further continuity is worth naming. The Old Testament pattern of the Spirit equipping ordinary people for real, tangible tasks did not disappear at Pentecost. It expanded, now available continuously rather than selectively. He is present in the skill you bring to your trade, the courage you summon in a difficult leadership moment, the strength you draw on to persevere.

Discussion Questions

  1. Read Psalm 51:11 in light of what happened to Saul. Explain why David's plea was rational rather than dramatic, and then explain why the same plea is not the appropriate prayer for a believer today.
  2. Bezalel was filled with the Spirit for craftsmanship. What does that widen in your expectation of where the Spirit is active in your own week?
  3. Explain the phrase from John 7:39 that the Spirit had not yet been given because Jesus was not yet glorified. Why does that clause rule out treating the Spirit as a second blessing?
  4. Pentecost reverses Babel. Work out what that implies for the church's posture toward cultural and linguistic difference.
  5. Name a specific way you have seen Old Testament patterns of the Spirit's activity imported into New Covenant teaching. What harm did it do?

Reflection

Read Psalm 51 slowly, noticing the desperation behind David's request. Then remember that you serve under different terms entirely. Write one sentence naming something you have been anxiously trying to keep that has, in fact, already been permanently secured for you.

Summary

The Spirit was never absent from Israel's story. He hovered over the waters at creation, filled Bezalel with practical skill, clothed Gideon with courage, gave Samson strength, and carried the prophets along as they spoke. Yet the pattern was selective, tied to task and moment, and it could be withdrawn, as Saul discovered and David feared. Even within the Old Testament, Moses longed for more, and Ezekiel, Jeremiah, and Joel promised it: a new heart, a law written inside, and an outpouring on all flesh. Jesus tied the fulfillment directly to His own glorification, which means the indwelling Spirit is secured by the cross rather than earned afterward. At Pentecost the promise arrived, on ordinary people, reversing Babel and inaugurating an age in which every believer receives the Spirit permanently. What changed was not the Spirit's character but the terms of His dwelling. Lesson 3 examines the first thing He does in a person.

There is a phrase that appears again and again throughout the New Testament, spoken with an urgency that never fades no matter how many centuries pass between its writing and our reading of it. He is coming back.

Not as a metaphor for God's ongoing presence in the world, not as a symbol for the spread of the gospel or the death of believers, but as an actual, visible, personal, bodily return of Jesus Christ to the earth He once walked, died on, and rose from. This lesson takes up that promise, and then the resurrection that Christianity stands or falls on.

:::objectives By the end of this topic you will be able to
1. Defend the visible, personal, bodily character of Christ's return from Acts 1 and Matthew 24.
2. Explain *parousia* and *apokalypsis* and what each contributes.
3. Explain Paul's pastoral purpose in 1 Thessalonians 4 and what the passage does and does not describe.
4. Answer the scoffers of 2 Peter 3 in Peter's own terms.
5. Explain why date-setting undermines the watchfulness Jesus commands.
6. Explain what the parables of the ten virgins and the talents teach about the delay.
7. Explain the firstfruits argument of 1 Corinthians 15 and why Paul stakes everything on it.
8. Describe the resurrected body using Paul's contrasts, and state what *spiritual body* does not mean.
9. Explain why the resurrection is universal in scope though its outcomes diverge.
10. State the practical conclusion Paul draws from the resurrection in 1 Corinthians 15:58.
:::

## Key Terms

:::terms
Parousia | Greek, arrival or presence | Used in the ancient world for the visit of a king to a city, an occasion of celebration and honour; applied to Christ's return as a royal arrival.
Apokalypsis | Greek, unveiling | Emphasising that what is now hidden, Christ's full glory and authority, will be openly displayed for all to see.
Blessed hope | | Titus 2:13, the appearing of the glory of our great God and Saviour Jesus Christ.
Firstfruits | | The earliest portion of a harvest brought to God as a sign and guarantee that the rest is coming; applied to Christ's resurrection.
Spiritual body | | Paul's phrase in 1 Corinthians 15:44. Not an immaterial body but a perfected one, no longer subject to decay and fully responsive to the Spirit.
Rapio | Latin, to seize or catch up | The term behind the English *rapture*, from Paul's description of believers caught up to meet the Lord.
:::

## 1. The Promise Made Before He Left

Acts records the scene on the Mount of Olives: the disciples watching Jesus ascend into a cloud that received Him out of their sight, straining their eyes upward, when two men in white robes appear beside them with a pointed question. Why do you stand looking into heaven. This Jesus, who was taken up from you into heaven, will come in the same way as you saw Him go.

Notice the specificity. Not a spiritual return, not a metaphorical presence, but the same Jesus, coming in the same way, visibly and bodily, exactly as He left.

Jesus described the event Himself in unmistakable terms. The sun darkened, the moon giving no light, the stars falling, the powers of the heavens shaken. Then the sign of the Son of Man appearing, and all the tribes of the earth mourning, and they will see Him coming on the clouds with power and great glory.

:::quote Chapter Five
This is cosmic language, describing an event that will not be quiet, private, or ambiguous. It will be visible to the whole earth at once, unmistakable in its scale and unmistakable in its meaning.
:::

## 2. Two Words for One Event

| Word | What it emphasises |
|---|---|
| *Parousia* | Arrival or presence, used for a king's visit to a city, marked by celebration and honour |
| *Apokalypsis* | Unveiling, emphasising that hidden glory will finally be openly displayed |

Together they picture the return as both royal arrival and public unveiling: the One who came the first time in humility, born in a stable and executed on a cross, returning in the fullness of a glory that has always belonged to Him but has not yet been fully revealed to a watching world.

## 3. Paul's Pastoral Purpose

The fullest description comes in 1 Thessalonians, and it is important to know why Paul wrote it. Believers were grieving over church members who had died before Christ's return, worried that those who had passed away would somehow miss the glory of that day.

Paul's answer is emphatic. The Lord Himself will descend from heaven with a cry of command, with the voice of an archangel, and with the sound of the trumpet of God. And the dead in Christ will rise **first**. Then those who are alive will be caught up together with them to meet the Lord in the air.

:::note Not a quiet disappearance
The passage is often referred to using the Latin *rapio*, to be caught up. Whatever else it describes, it is not a secret vanishing. It is a triumphant reunion accompanied by trumpet blasts and shouted commands, the language of a royal arrival rather than a quiet escape.
:::

## 4. Answering the Scoffers

Peter addresses those who mock the delay, asking where the promise of His coming has gone since everything continues as it was from the beginning.

His response is worth sitting with. With the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day. The Lord is not slow to fulfil His promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish but that all should reach repentance.

:::quote Chapter Five
The delay, however long it may feel to those of us living inside history, is not evidence of God's forgetfulness. It is evidence of his mercy, giving space for repentance.
:::

## 5. The Discipline of Not Calculating

Jesus said plainly that of that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels of heaven, nor the Son, but the Father only. Given how directly that comes from Jesus Himself, it should settle the question for anyone tempted to calculate.

:::warn What date-setting actually costs
Every generation that has attempted such calculations has been proven wrong, and every attempt has undermined the very posture of watchful readiness Jesus calls for. He does not want us calculating. He wants us watching, the way a servant watches for a master who could arrive at any hour, staying faithful to the tasks assigned rather than staring at a calendar.
:::

Two parables press the point from different angles. The ten virgins turns entirely on preparedness: the foolish, caught without enough oil when the bridegroom arrived unexpectedly, found themselves shut out. The talents commends the servants who invested what their master entrusted to them and condemns the one who buried his out of fear. Both teach the same lesson in different clothing. The delay is not an excuse for spiritual laziness. It is an opportunity for faithful stewardship.

## 6. Vindication for a Suffering Church

The return carries an unmistakable note of vindication. Paul tells the Thessalonians that it is just for God to repay with affliction those who afflict you, and to grant relief to you who are afflicted, when the Lord Jesus is revealed from heaven with His mighty angels in flaming fire.

The suffering church throughout history, and in our own day, does not suffer forever and does not suffer unseen.

Revelation closes the canon with the same hope restated directly. Jesus says, I am coming soon. The last words spoken by any human voice in Scripture are John's, echoed by the Spirit and the bride: come. Amen. Come, Lord Jesus.

:::note On the disputed details
Faithful, careful students of the Bible have reached different conclusions about the relationship between Christ's return and the tribulation, and about the sequence connecting His return to the thousand-year reign of Revelation 20. This textbook intentionally declines to stake out a position where informed believers continue to disagree. What unites every faithful view, and what deserves far more attention than the details that divide them, is the single unshakable centre: Jesus Christ is coming back, visibly, personally, and gloriously, to finish what He began at the cross and the empty tomb.
:::

## 7. The Claim Christianity Stands or Falls On

Paul does not hedge. If Christ has not been raised, he tells the Corinthians, our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain. He goes further: if the dead are not raised at all, then Christ has not been raised, and if Christ has not been raised your faith is futile, you are still in your sins, and those who have died in Christ have perished.

This is not rhetorical exaggeration. Paul genuinely believes the entire structure of the faith depends on a bodily, historical resurrection.

But he does not leave the matter in doubt. In fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep.

:::quote Chapter Six
That word firstfruits is agricultural, drawn from the practice of bringing the earliest portion of a harvest to God as a sign and guarantee that the rest of the harvest is coming.
:::

Jesus' resurrection is not presented as an isolated miracle performed on one man. It is the first installment of a much larger resurrection, the guarantee that everyone united to Him by faith will follow Him through the same door He opened. He makes the claim directly to Martha: I am the resurrection and the life. Notice that He does not merely promise resurrection as a future event. He identifies Himself as the resurrection, meaning that resurrection life is something believers already possess in union with Him.

## 8. What the Resurrected Body Will Be

Paul anticipates the Corinthian objection that a bodily resurrection sounds implausible, and answers with a seed. What is sown is not the body that is to be, but a bare kernel. God gives it a body as He has chosen.

The comparison teaches continuity. An oak is genuinely continuous with the acorn even though the mature tree looks nothing like the seed. Your resurrected body will be genuinely yours, continuous with the body you have now, and yet transformed beyond anything that body currently is.

| Sown | Raised |
|---|---|
| Perishable | Imperishable |
| In dishonour | In glory |
| In weakness | In power |
| A natural body | A spiritual body |

:::warn What *spiritual body* does not mean
It does not mean immaterial or ghostlike. Paul is describing not the loss of physicality but its perfection: a body no longer subject to decay, sickness, fatigue, or death, fully responsive to the Holy Spirit rather than limited by our present weakness.
:::

The resurrected Jesus confirms it. He ate broiled fish in front of His disciples. He invited Thomas to touch the wounds in His hands and side. He was recognisably physical even as He demonstrated abilities beyond what His pre-resurrection body possessed, appearing in locked rooms without needing a door. Whatever else the resurrected body will be, it will be a real, physical, tangible body, just like His. Philippians makes the pattern explicit: He will transform our lowly body to be like His glorious body.

## 9. Timing and Scope

Paul ties the resurrection directly to the return described earlier in this lesson. Behold, I tell you a mystery. We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed, in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet.

This is the same event as 1 Thessalonians 4: the dead in Christ rising to meet the returning Lord, joined by believers still alive, all instantaneously transformed. It is not a gradual process stretched over years but a single decisive event, sudden and complete.

Scripture also describes a resurrection of **all** people, not only believers. Jesus states it plainly: an hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear His voice and come out, those who have done good to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgment. Daniel anticipates the same reality centuries earlier. Resurrection is universal in scope even though its outcomes diverge completely, depending entirely on a person's relationship to Christ. Topic 4 takes up that divergence.

## 10. Why It Changes Tuesday

After the soaring description of victory over death, Paul does not end with a poetic flourish about the world to come. He ends with an exhortation about the present. Therefore, my beloved brothers, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that in the Lord your labour is not in vain.

If the dead are not raised, he had argued earlier, then eat and drink, for tomorrow we die, would be the only rational philosophy. But because the dead are raised, because your body itself has a future, nothing done in faithfulness to Christ, however small or hidden, is ever wasted.

:::quote Chapter Six
The believer confined to a wheelchair, the believer whose mind has been robbed by disease, the believer whose body has been marked by violence or accident or the ordinary cruelty of illness, all of them are held by the same promise given to every other believer, that what is sown in weakness will be raised in power.
:::

This doctrine also refuses every philosophy that treats the physical world as unimportant or disposable. God made bodies. Christ rose in a body. The ordinary discipline of caring for the body you have now, honouring it rather than despising it, gains weight when you remember that this body, transformed but continuous, is the very body you will inhabit forever.

## Summary

The return of Christ is visible, personal, and bodily, promised by the angels at the ascension in exactly those terms and described by Jesus in cosmic language no one could mistake. *Parousia* names it a royal arrival and *apokalypsis* an unveiling of glory now hidden. Paul's fullest description was written to comfort grieving people, and it is a triumphant reunion rather than a quiet vanishing. Peter reframes the delay as mercy, Jesus forbids calculation, and two parables convert waiting into stewardship. On the disputed sequences the textbook deliberately holds its peace, keeping the centre in view. The resurrection is the claim the faith stands or falls on, and Christ's is the firstfruits guaranteeing the harvest. The body raised is continuous with the body sown but perfected, physical rather than ghostly, patterned on the risen Christ who ate fish and showed His wounds. All the dead will rise, though to opposite outcomes. And the practical conclusion Paul draws is not speculation but steadfastness: in the Lord your labour is not in vain. Topic 4 turns to the judgment that follows.

Every human being carries a deep and sometimes unspoken conviction that justice matters, that wrongs deserve to be answered, that a universe where cruelty and kindness receive exactly the same outcome would be intolerable to live in. That instinct did not arise from nowhere.

Scripture teaches that it reflects something true about God's own character. History is not an endless cycle without accountability. It is moving toward a day when every account will be settled with perfect accuracy, and this lesson works carefully through what Scripture says that day will involve.

:::objectives By the end of this topic you will be able to
1. Explain the connection Paul draws in Acts 17 between the resurrection and the certainty of judgment.
2. Explain why the identity of the Judge is a comfort as much as a warning.
3. Explain the function of the criteria in Matthew 25 without turning them into a system of earning salvation.
4. Define the *bema* and state precisely what is and is not being evaluated there.
5. Explain the gold, silver, and precious stones image of 1 Corinthians 3 and the loss it describes.
6. Describe the great white throne judgment and the decisive role of the book of life.
7. Answer the question of how works can function at judgment if salvation is by faith alone.
8. Explain where a believer's confidence for the day of judgment actually rests.
9. Explain why a God who did not judge sin would not be a God worth trusting.
:::

## 1. The Resurrection Guarantees the Judgment

Paul describes the coming day directly in his sermon at Athens, telling the philosophers that God has fixed a day on which He will judge the world in righteousness by a man whom He has appointed, and of this He has given assurance to all by raising Him from the dead.

Notice the connection. The same historical event that guarantees believers will be raised also guarantees that judgment is coming, and it guarantees the identity of the Judge.

:::quote Chapter Seven
This is a comfort as much as it is a warning, because the one who will judge the world is not a distant or unfamiliar figure. He is the one who bled for sinners, who understands human weakness from the inside, and whose own character defines what perfect justice actually looks like.
:::

It should also give pause to anyone tempted to think of Jesus only in terms of gentleness. He speaks about coming judgment more often than almost any other subject in the Gospels, as though gentleness and a commitment to justice were never in tension to begin with.

## 2. The Sheep and the Goats

His most extended teaching comes in Matthew 25. The Son of Man comes in His glory with all the angels, sits on His glorious throne, and before Him all the nations are gathered, and He separates people one from another as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats.

The criteria He names are concrete: feeding the hungry, welcoming the stranger, clothing the naked, visiting the sick and imprisoned.

:::warn How not to read Matthew 25
These are not presented as a new system of earning salvation through good works. They function as evidence, the visible fruit revealing whether a genuine relationship with Christ exists within a person's heart. As Jesus puts it, as you did it to one of the least of these My brothers, you did it to Me.
:::

## 3. Two Judgments, Carefully Distinguished

Much confusion on this subject dissolves once two distinct scenes are held apart.

| | Judgment seat of Christ | Great white throne |
|---|---|---|
| Who appears | Believers | Those who have not trusted Christ |
| Question at issue | The quality of works done after salvation | The record of a life, measured against the book of life |
| What is at stake | Reward, and possible loss of it | Eternal destiny |
| Already settled | Salvation, through faith in Christ | Nothing |
| Key text | 2 Corinthians 5:10; 1 Corinthians 3:10 to 15 | Revelation 20:11 to 15 |

## 4. The Bema

Paul writes that we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may receive what is due for what he has done in the body, whether good or evil.

The Greek word is *bema*, drawn from the athletic games of the ancient world, referring to the raised platform where a judge awarded prizes to victorious competitors. That background matters.

:::quote Chapter Seven
Believers do not appear at this judgment to determine whether they are saved. That question has already been settled through faith in Christ. Rather, this judgment evaluates the quality of a believer's works, done after salvation, for the purpose of reward.
:::

Paul develops the image in 1 Corinthians. Some build on the foundation with gold, silver, and precious stones; others with wood, hay, and straw. The fire of that day will test what sort of work each one has done. If the work survives, the builder receives a reward. If it is burned up, he suffers loss, though he himself will be saved, but only as through fire.

:::note The loss is real, and so is the limit
That is a sobering possibility, and it should not be softened. A believer may arrive with very little that survives the fire. But even that loss is bounded by the certainty of his salvation. It concerns the reward for a life already secured, not the security of that life itself.
:::

## 5. The Great White Throne

A different and more severe judgment awaits those who have not trusted Christ. John describes it: a great white throne and Him who sat on it, from whose presence earth and sky fled away. The dead, great and small, stand before the throne, and books are opened. Another book is opened, which is the book of life. And the dead are judged by what is written in the books, according to what they had done.

This is judgment according to works, and Scripture is clear that measured by our own works none of us stand any chance of a favourable verdict, since all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God, and the wages of sin is death.

This is precisely why the mention of the book of life matters so much. Anyone not found written in the book of life was thrown into the lake of fire. The deciding factor is not merely a record of deeds but whether one's name appears in that book, a book Revelation elsewhere describes as belonging to the Lamb who was slain, meaning that inclusion comes only through faith in His atoning death.

## 6. The Question That Deserves an Honest Answer

If salvation comes through faith alone, why does judgment involve an evaluation of works at all?

Scripture answers by presenting works not as the grounds of salvation but as the evidence of it. James makes the argument forcefully, insisting that faith apart from works is dead, not because works earn salvation but because genuine faith inevitably produces them, the way a healthy tree inevitably produces fruit.

| At judgment, works function as | For the believer | For the unbeliever |
|---|---|---|
| The visible record of the heart | Evidence of a life transformed by grace, supporting a verdict already secured by faith | Confirmation, alongside the absence of a name in the book of life, of a life lived apart from Christ |

## 7. Where a Believer's Confidence Actually Rests

This subject carries real emotional weight, particularly for sensitive believers who wonder whether they have done enough, or who feel anxious rereading the sheep and the goats.

Scripture addresses that anxiety not by lowering the standard of judgment but by pointing believers back to the sufficiency of Christ's finished work. There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. That *therefore* is doing enormous work in the sentence, following as it does from an extended argument about justification by faith.

:::quote Chapter Seven
The believer facing the final judgment does not stand there hoping his good deeds will outweigh his bad ones on some cosmic scale. He stands there clothed in the righteousness of Christ, a righteousness not his own, received entirely as a gift, sufficient to answer every accusation that could ever be brought against him.
:::

This is why John can write, with striking confidence, that love is perfected with us so that we may have confidence for the day of judgment, because as He is, so also are we in this world. The believer's confidence is not confidence in the sufficiency of his own record. It is confidence in his union with the One whose record is perfect.

## 8. Without Softening the Other Side

None of this diminishes the seriousness of the judgment awaiting those who reject Christ, and Scripture never softens that reality to make it more comfortable. Paul warns the Thessalonians that the Lord Jesus will be revealed from heaven, inflicting vengeance on those who do not know God and do not obey the gospel, who will suffer the punishment of eternal destruction, away from the presence of the Lord and from the glory of His might.

This should be read soberly, not as a threat wielded to manipulate but as an honest description of the stakes involved in how a person responds to the gospel, stakes that should give every believer genuine urgency in sharing the good news.

## 9. A Doctrine About God's Character

Taken as a whole, the final judgment is ultimately a doctrine about God, and specifically about the perfect union of His justice and His mercy.

A God who did not judge sin would not be a God worth trusting, because a universe of unaccountable cruelty is not a universe any of us actually want to live in, however much we might wish to escape accountability for our own failures. But a God whose justice offered no path of mercy would leave every one of us without hope, since none could survive an evaluation based purely on merit.

:::quote Chapter Seven
The gospel is the announcement that God has provided exactly the path of mercy his justice requires, satisfying the full weight of judgment against sin in the death of his own Son, so that everyone who trusts in Christ can stand before the final judgment not in terror but in confident hope, knowing that the verdict has already been rendered in their favor at the cross, long before that final day ever arrives.
:::

## Summary

The instinct that wrongs must be answered reflects something true about God, and Paul grounds the certainty of the coming day in the resurrection itself, which also names the Judge: the One who bled for sinners and understands weakness from the inside. Matthew 25 supplies concrete criteria that function as evidence of a real relationship rather than as a system for earning one. Two scenes must be held apart. At the *bema*, believers whose salvation is already settled have the quality of their work tested by fire, with real reward and real loss at stake but never their security. At the great white throne, the books are opened and the decisive question is whether a name appears in the book of life, since no record of deeds could ever secure a favourable verdict. Works therefore serve as evidence at both, confirming for the believer what faith has secured and exposing for the unbeliever what was never there. The believer's confidence rests entirely in union with the One whose record is perfect, which is why Romans 8:1 can say there is no condemnation at all. The doctrine finally describes God Himself, whose justice makes Him trustworthy and whose mercy makes Him approachable. Topic 5 turns to the two destinies this judgment assigns.

Ask most people what they imagine heaven to be like and you will hear a strange assortment of images borrowed from cartoons and folklore rather than Scripture. Clouds, harps, wings, an eternity of vague floating. It is little wonder that many sincere believers find themselves privately unmoved by the promise, half suspecting it might turn out to be a pleasant but unexciting reward.

The second half of this lesson is the most difficult material in the course, and it should be. No honest teacher approaches the subject of hell with anything other than sober reluctance, and any presentation that seems eager or comfortable has almost certainly misunderstood something essential about it.

:::objectives By the end of this lesson you will be able to
1. State what heaven is fundamentally defined by, and support it from John 14.
2. Correct the popular picture of heaven from the specific images of Revelation 21 and 22.
3. Explain the theological significance of the tree of life and of the absence of a temple.
4. Describe what Scripture indicates believers will actually do in eternity.
5. Explain how Colossians 3 and Matthew 6 turn the hope of heaven into a reordering of present priorities.
6. Explain why hell exists, in terms of God's holiness rather than divine vindictiveness.
7. Identify the images Jesus uses for hell and what each emphasises, including the background of Gehenna.
8. State the theological heart of hell using 2 Thessalonians 1:9.
9. Give an honest answer to the objection that eternal punishment does not fit a finite life of sin.
10. Describe the posture this doctrine should produce in a believer, and the posture it must never produce.
:::

## Key Terms

:::terms

New Jerusalem | | The city John sees coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband.
Tree of life | | The Eden image restored and multiplied in Revelation 22, bearing fruit continuously rather than seasonally, its leaves for the healing of the nations.
Gehenna | Greek, from the Valley of Hinnom | The valley outside Jerusalem with a history of child sacrifice, later a place of continually burning refuse; Jesus' most visceral image for eternal judgment.
Second death | | Revelation's phrase for the lake of fire, describing not annihilation but ongoing conscious separation.
Wrath of God | | Not an uncontrolled divine temper but the settled, righteous response of a holy God to genuine evil.

## 1. Heaven Is Defined by a Person

At its foundation, heaven is not primarily a place defined by its scenery, however glorious that scenery may be. It is the place of unbroken, unmediated fellowship with God Himself, and everything else Scripture says about heaven flows outward from that centre.

On the night before His crucifixion, comforting disciples about to lose Him, Jesus said: let not your hearts be troubled. In My Father's house are many rooms. If I go and prepare a place for you, I will come again and will take you to Myself, that where I am you may be also.

:::quote Chapter Eight
Notice what Jesus identifies as the actual substance of the promise. Not a description of the architecture of the place, but a promise of his own presence. Where I am you may be also. That is the heart of heaven.
:::

Paul understood this so completely that he could call departing this life to be with Christ *far better* than remaining, even though remaining meant fruitful ministry that mattered enormously. He did not long for heaven because he was tired of earthly life. He longed for heaven because he longed for Christ.

## 2. Correcting the Popular Picture

Revelation gives the most extensive description of the eternal state anywhere in Scripture, and its images are chosen with theological precision rather than poetic flourish.

| Image | What it corrects or establishes |
|---|---|
| God's dwelling place with man | A reunion of heaven and earth, not an escape from one to the other |
| Every tear wiped away; no death, mourning, crying, or pain | The precise wounds sin inflicted, each named and undone |
| A city, the new Jerusalem, adorned as a bride | Covenant intimacy, tying eternity to the marriage language running through Scripture |
| Foundations of every precious stone; radiance like a rare jewel | Beauty on a scale the popular version never imagines |
| Gates never shut, for there is no night; nations bringing their glory | Overflowing life, welcome, and activity rather than emptiness |

This is not souls floating disembodied in an ethereal realm removed from physical reality. It is a city bustling with the redeemed from every tribe and tongue, gathered in the presence of the God who made them.

## 3. The Tree, and the Missing Temple

Two details carry more weight than their brevity suggests.

The tree of life stands on either side of the river of the water of life, bearing twelve kinds of fruit, yielding its fruit each month, its leaves for the healing of the nations. This draws a direct line back to Eden, where access to the tree of life was cut off after sin entered. Revelation shows that access restored, and more than restored, multiplied: fruit continuously rather than seasonally, leaves offering healing to a creation finally free of the wounds sin inflicted.

:::quote Chapter Eight
The story of Scripture, read from beginning to end, is the story of a garden lost and a city gained, a paradise regained not as a return to a simpler past but as an advance into a more glorious future.
:::

John also tells us he saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb. Throughout the Old Testament the temple existed as the place where a holy God could dwell among a sinful people, mediated through priests, sacrifices, and careful ritual. In the new creation that mediation is no longer needed, not because God lowered His standard of holiness but because sin itself has been removed from His people, who now stand in His direct presence without a single barrier remaining.

## 4. What Believers Will Actually Do

Scripture suggests an eternity that is anything but idle. Revelation describes the redeemed serving God continually, worshipping in a way that clearly involves active, engaged participation rather than passive floating. It describes believers reigning with Christ, language suggesting genuine responsibility and purposeful activity. It describes a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation and tribe and people and language, standing before the throne.

:::note What heaven is not
Heaven is not the absence of activity, purpose, relationship, or meaning. It is the fulfilment of everything good those things were always meant to be, finally free of the frustration, fatigue, and disappointment that mar even our best experiences of them now.
:::

Nor is it solitary. The picture throughout Revelation is thoroughly communal, and Paul comforted the Thessalonians with exactly the expectation of reunion with believers who had died before them. Whatever specific form that recognition takes, Scripture gives every reason to expect that relationships forged in this life through shared faith are not erased in the next but gathered up, purified, and enjoyed forever.

And one feature is easy to overlook amid the jewelled foundations: heaven will be entirely free of sin, not merely a place where sin is forgiven but a place where its very possibility has been removed. An eternity without the friction, suspicion, selfishness, and hidden hurt that mark even our best relationships now.

## 5. What This Hope Is For

This hope was never intended to function as an escape hatch from present difficulty, though it certainly does comfort in suffering. It was intended to reorder present priorities entirely.

Paul instructs the Colossians to seek the things above, where Christ is, and to set their minds there rather than on things on earth. This is not a call to neglect earthly responsibilities, since the same letter is filled with detailed practical instruction for exactly those relationships. It is a call to hold every earthly thing loosely in light of a heavier and more lasting reality.

Jesus makes the same point: do not lay up treasures on earth where moth and rust destroy, but in heaven, where they do not. For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.

:::quote Chapter Eight
What you treasure determines what you love, and what you love determines how you live. The believer who has genuinely grasped the reality of heaven finds it easier, not harder, to hold this present world with open hands.
:::

## 6. Why Hell Exists

Jesus, the same Jesus known for compassion toward the outcast and gentleness toward the broken, spoke about hell more frequently and more vividly than any other figure in the Bible. If we trust Him regarding heaven, we owe Him the same trust regarding this harder teaching.

It helps to begin with why hell exists at all, because the doctrine is often presented in a way that makes God sound arbitrary, sentencing people to eternal torment for comparatively minor offences. Scripture presents something different. Sin, however small it may appear from a human vantage point, is fundamentally an offence against an infinitely holy God, and God's holiness cannot simply overlook sin without ceasing to be holy. Habakkuk describes God as having eyes too pure to look at evil.

:::quote Chapter Nine
This is not a flaw in God's character. It is the very thing that makes him trustworthy. The same perfect justice that guarantees every wrong will eventually be set right also guarantees that sin cannot simply be waved away as though it did not matter.
:::

Paul writes that the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness. This wrath is not, as it is sometimes caricatured, an uncontrolled divine temper tantrum. It is the settled, righteous response of a holy God to genuine evil, the same kind of response any morally serious person feels toward cruelty and oppression, except perfect, proportionate, and entirely untainted by the pettiness or excess that corrupts human anger.

## 7. The Images, and What Each Emphasises

| Image | What it emphasises |
|---|---|
| Outer darkness, weeping and gnashing of teeth | Complete exclusion from the light and warmth of God's presence |
| Fire that is not quenched, where the worm does not die | Ongoing, unrelenting judgment rather than temporary or purifying punishment |
| Gehenna | The Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, with its grim history of child sacrifice and later use as a place of continually burning refuse |
| The lake of fire, the second death | Continuation of conscious existence permanently separated from everything good |

When Jesus wanted His listeners to grasp the horror of eternal judgment, He reached for the most visceral image available to them, a valley of perpetual burning associated with the worst evils their history had known.

:::warn Not annihilation
Scripture consistently describes hell as ongoing, conscious experience rather than the simple ceasing to exist, however comforting that alternative interpretation might seem to some. The phrase *second death* captures something about its nature: not extinction, but a continuation of existence separated permanently from the presence of God and from the light and life He alone provides.
:::

## 8. The Theological Heart of It

Paul offers what may be the clearest single summary: those who do not obey the gospel suffer the punishment of eternal destruction, **away from the presence of the Lord** and from the glory of His might.

That phrase gets at the heart of hell more precisely than any of the vivid imagery surrounding it. Every good thing human beings experience, every pleasure, every relationship, every moment of beauty or meaning or joy, exists only because God in His common grace allows His goodness to reach even a rebellious creation.

:::quote Chapter Nine
Hell represents the complete and final withdrawal of that goodness, the natural and inevitable consequence of a soul that has, throughout its earthly life, insisted on independence from God, finally granted that independence in full and discovering too late what a horror it actually is to be entirely separated from the only source of everything good.
:::

## 9. The Hardest Question, Answered Honestly

Does eternal punishment fit the crime of a finite human lifetime of sin? This deserves a serious answer rather than a dismissive one, and Scripture's response has three parts.

1. Sin against an infinite and eternally holy God carries a weight that a merely human calculation of finite offences cannot capture.
2. The punishment described is not merely for actions committed during a single lifetime but for an ongoing posture of rebellion that, left unaddressed by grace, would continue indefinitely were it given the chance.
3. Our own moral instincts, however useful, are not the final court of appeal for judging God's justice.

Abraham, wrestling with God over the fate of Sodom, appealed to God's own character rather than to his own moral intuitions, asking, shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just. That question, asked in humility rather than accusation, remains the right posture for believers wrestling honestly with the hard edges of this doctrine.

## 10. The Character of the God Who Reveals It

What should never be lost is the heart behind the warning. Peter says the Lord is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish but that all should reach repentance. Ezekiel records God asking directly whether He has any pleasure in the death of the wicked, and not rather that he should turn from his way and live.

:::quote Chapter Nine
Every warning Scripture gives about hell is given, ultimately, as an act of mercy, a flare sent up to warn travelers away from a cliff before they reach its edge.
:::

Jesus wept over Jerusalem, a city that would soon reject Him, longing to gather its people the way a hen gathers her brood, and they were not willing. That grief, that patient mercy extended right to the final moment, is the true heart behind every hard word Scripture speaks about judgment.

:::warn The posture this doctrine must never produce
It should never produce satisfaction or superiority toward those who reject Christ, and any teacher who speaks about hell with anything resembling glee has badly misunderstood both the doctrine and the heart of the God who revealed it. It should produce urgency, the same urgency Paul felt when he wrote of great sorrow and unceasing anguish for his fellow Israelites. It should produce compassion, gratitude for one's own rescue, and a deepened appreciation for the enormity of what Christ absorbed at the cross. Rightly held, this doctrine does not make a person harsher. It makes the gospel sound louder, more urgent, and more precious than it ever did before.
:::

No one who ends up separated from God forever does so because God failed to warn them or failed to provide a way of escape. The same book that describes outer darkness also describes, on nearly every page, a God who runs to meet returning sons while they are still far off, who leaves the ninety-nine to search for the one, who stands at the door and knocks.

## Reflection

Read Revelation 21:3 to 5 aloud, slowly. Then name the specific tear you most want wiped away, and sit with the fact that the promise is personal.

## Summary

Heaven is defined by a Person before it is defined by a place, which is why Jesus grounds the promise in *where I am you may be also* and why Paul could call departure far better than fruitful ministry. Revelation corrects the folklore version at every point: a dwelling of God with man rather than an escape from earth, a bustling city rather than an empty sky, tears named and wiped away, a tree of life restored and multiplied beyond Eden, and no temple at all because the mediation it provided is no longer needed. Eternity is active, communal, and free not only of forgiven sin but of sin's very possibility, and this hope is meant to reorder present treasure rather than provide an escape from present difficulty. Hell exists because a holy God cannot overlook sin without ceasing to be holy, and Jesus, who spoke of it more than anyone, used images of exclusion, unquenched fire, and Gehenna to convey what separation means. Its theological heart is the withdrawal of the presence from which every good thing has always come. The objection about proportionality gets an honest three-part answer rather than a dismissal, and the God who reveals the doctrine is the same God who takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked and wept over the city that rejected Him. Held rightly, this doctrine makes the gospel louder, not the believer harsher. Lesson 6 turns to the new creation, and to the life this hope requires today.

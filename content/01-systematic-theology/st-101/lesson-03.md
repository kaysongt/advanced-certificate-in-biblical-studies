Owning a trustworthy Bible is only the beginning. The next question every serious believer must wrestle with is how to actually read it.

Dr. Kay is blunt about why this matters. He has watched well-meaning Christians take verses wildly out of context, build entire doctrines on a single obscure passage while ignoring the clear teaching of Scripture elsewhere, or read New Testament meaning back into Old Testament texts in ways the original authors never intended. None of this happens because people love the Bible too little. It usually happens because they were never taught how to interpret it carefully.

The discipline is called hermeneutics. Paul urges Timothy to be diligent to present himself approved to God, a worker who does not need to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth (2 Timothy 2:15). Notice that phrase, *rightly dividing*. It implies Scripture can be handled wrongly, cut against its own grain.

:::objectives By the end of this topic you will be able to
1. State and apply the principle that context governs meaning, including historical, literary, and cultural context.
2. Identify the major biblical genres and explain why genre determines how a passage is read.
3. Apply the analogy of Scripture, using clearer passages to interpret less clear ones.
4. Read a passage within its covenantal setting and avoid both legalism and dismissal of the Old Testament.
5. Explain and defend the Christ-centered reading of the whole Bible from Luke 24.
6. Trace the storyline of redemption through its major movements and locate a given passage within it.
:::

## Key Scriptures

:::scripture Luke 24:27
And beginning at Moses and all the Prophets, He expounded to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning Himself.
:::

This is the single most important interpretive precedent in the Bible, because the interpreter is Jesus and the subject is the entire Old Testament. He did not merely explain a handful of messianic prophecies. He treated all of it as concerning Himself.

- **2 Timothy 2:15** — rightly dividing the word of truth
- **Psalm 91:4** — poetic imagery that must be read as poetry
- **Acts 17:11** — the Bereans searching the Scriptures daily

## Key Terms

:::terms
Hermeneutics | Greek *hermeneuein*, to interpret | The science and art of biblical interpretation.
Exegesis | Greek, to lead out | Drawing out of a text what is actually there, as opposed to reading into it what is not.
Genre | | The literary type of a passage. Scripture contains narrative, poetry, wisdom, prophecy, gospel, epistle, and apocalyptic, each with its own conventions.
Analogy of Scripture | | The conviction that Scripture is its own best interpreter, so that clearer passages govern our reading of less clear ones.
Covenant | | A binding relationship established by God with His people. Scripture moves through several, from Adam and Noah through Abraham, Moses, and David, culminating in the new covenant in Christ.
Protoevangelium | Latin, first gospel | Genesis 3:15, where God announces that the offspring of the woman will bruise the serpent's head.
Descriptive and Prescriptive | | The distinction between a passage that reports what happened and one that commands what should be done.
:::

## 1. Context Governs Meaning

This is the single most important interpretive principle. Every verse in the Bible was written by a real author, to a real audience, in a real historical moment, using a particular literary form, addressing a particular situation.

:::quote Chapter Four
A verse pulled from its paragraph is like a single brick pulled from a building. You can hold the brick in your hand and admire it, but you cannot understand what it was built to support without seeing the structure around it.
:::

The order of questions matters. Before asking what a passage means for us today, we must first ask what it meant to its original audience. Only after answering that can we responsibly ask how the meaning applies now.

Historical and cultural context does real work here. When Paul writes to the Corinthians about church order, he is addressing a specific congregation wrestling with specific problems in a Greco-Roman city known for moral chaos and religious pluralism. When the prophets speak of coming judgment, they are often addressing specific covenant violations by Israel or Judah at a particular moment. Understanding this background does not diminish the Bible's relevance. It sharpens it, by helping us distinguish principles that transcend every culture from applications shaped by circumstances unique to the original audience.

## 2. Genre Determines Reading

The Bible is not one uniform type of literature. It contains historical narrative, poetry, wisdom literature, prophecy, gospel, epistle, and apocalyptic vision, and each genre has its own conventions for how meaning is communicated.

We do not read the poetic imagery of the Psalms the way we read the historical narrative of Kings, and we do not read the highly symbolic visions of Revelation the way we read the straightforward moral instruction of a Pauline epistle.

When the Psalmist writes that God will cover him with His feathers, and under His wings he shall take refuge (Psalm 91:4), we are not meant to imagine God as a literal bird. We are meant to feel the tenderness and protection that image conveys.

:::warn Two failures, opposite directions
Failing to recognize genre leads either to flattening rich poetic language into wooden literalism, or, just as dangerously, to spiritualizing straightforward historical narrative into something it was never meant to be.
:::

A related safeguard concerns narrative in particular. Readers often assume every detail within a story carries independent theological weight. When Scripture records that an Old Testament figure did something, it is not always endorsing that action as a pattern for us. Sometimes narrative simply reports what happened, whether wise or foolish, faithful or sinful, and leaves us to discern the passage's actual teaching point from its broader context and its place in the unfolding story.

## 3. The Analogy of Scripture

Because the entire Bible has one divine author working through many human authors, it does not contradict itself when rightly understood. Clearer passages should be used to help us understand less clear ones.

If we come across a difficult or ambiguous verse, our first move should not be to build a doctrine around our initial impression of it. Our first move should be to see what the rest of Scripture, especially the clearer and more direct passages, teaches on that subject.

The Bereans modeled this. Luke describes them as more noble than the Thessalonians, in that they received the word with all readiness, and searched the Scriptures daily to find out whether these things were so (Acts 17:11). Even when Paul himself was teaching them, they tested his words against the whole counsel of Scripture.

## 4. Covenantal Context

The Bible moves through several major covenants, beginning with the promises made to Adam and Eve, continuing through Noah, Abraham, Moses, and David, and culminating in the new covenant established through the blood of Jesus Christ.

Not every command given under a previous covenant applies directly to believers living under the new covenant in the same form. The dietary laws and sacrificial system given to Israel under Moses served real and important purposes in their original setting, and they find their fulfillment in Christ rather than continuing as binding requirements for the church.

Reading Scripture without sensitivity to this covenantal movement leads to one of two errors: a confused legalism that tries to reapply old covenant regulations wholesale, or an equally confused dismissal of the Old Testament as though it had nothing left to teach.

## 5. A Worked Example: Jeremiah 29:11

Consider the well-known verse where the Lord tells Jeremiah that He knows the plans He has for His people, plans for peace and not for evil, to give them a future and a hope. The verse is printed on graduation cards and framed on office walls, and is frequently applied as a general promise of personal prosperity to any individual believer in any circumstance.

Faithful interpretation asks what the passage meant first. Jeremiah wrote these words to Israelites carried into exile in Babylon, addressing their specific national circumstance and God's specific covenant promise to restore them to their land after seventy years.

:::quote Chapter Four
Recognizing this context does not strip the verse of relevance for us today. It actually deepens its meaning, revealing a God who remains faithful to His covenant purposes even through seasons of discipline and apparent absence.
:::

That pattern genuinely does extend to God's faithfulness toward His people in every generation, by legitimate theological principle rather than careless application, while still respecting what the passage originally and specifically addressed.

## 6. The Spirit and the Community

Faithful hermeneutics is never a purely mechanical exercise performed by human reasoning alone. Jesus promised His disciples that when the Spirit of truth came, He would guide them into all truth (John 16:13), and Paul reminds us that the natural person cannot understand spiritual truth apart from the Spirit's illuminating work (1 Corinthians 2:14). Careful method must be joined with humble, Spirit-dependent prayer.

Nor were we meant to interpret alone. Peter warns that some things in Paul's letters are hard to understand, and that untaught and unstable people twist them, as they do the rest of the Scriptures, to their own destruction (2 Peter 3:15 to 16). The Ethiopian official reading Isaiah gives the better model. When Philip asked whether he understood what he was reading, the official answered honestly, how can I, unless someone guides me, and welcomed Philip into the chariot (Acts 8:30 to 31).

Peter's warning should produce two responses at once. **Humility**, because our first impression of a difficult passage is not automatically correct. **Confidence**, because the fact that some passages are difficult does not mean the Bible as a whole is unclear. The central truths necessary for salvation and godly living are taught with remarkable clarity.

## 7. The Story: Creation, Fall, Promise

Everything above serves one final principle, and it is best understood by seeing the whole story at once.

The story begins in Genesis with creation, God speaking a good world into existence and placing humanity within it, bearing His image, entrusted with stewardship and relationship with Him. Humanity's original condition was not brokenness. It was blessing.

Genesis 3 records the fall, humanity's rebellion against God's clear command, and the catastrophic consequences that followed for the entire created order. Yet in the very moment of judgment, God spoke a promise that would echo across the rest of Scripture. Speaking to the serpent, God declared that He would put enmity between the serpent and the woman, and between the serpent's offspring and her offspring, and that her offspring would bruise the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15).

:::quote Chapter Five
This verse, sometimes called the protoevangelium, the first gospel, plants the seed of everything that follows. From humanity's darkest moment, God announced His intention to send a deliverer.
:::

## 8. The Story: Covenant, Failure, Exile, Anticipation

God called Abraham, promising to make him a great nation, to bless him, and to bless all the families of the earth through him (Genesis 12:1 to 3). That promise, given to one man, was never meant to stay confined to one man or one nation.

Through Abraham's descendants God formed Israel, delivered them from Egypt through Moses, established His covenant at Sinai, and gave them the law as the pattern for life as His covenant people. What follows is a story of repeated failure and repeated grace: conquest under Joshua, the cycles of the judges, monarchy under Saul, David, and Solomon, a divided kingdom spiraling into idolatry and injustice, and finally exile.

Even in exile, the prophets spoke words of both judgment and hope. Isaiah described a servant who would be wounded for our transgressions and bruised for our iniquities, by whose stripes we are healed (Isaiah 53:5), written centuries before crucifixion existed as a method of execution. Jeremiah described a new covenant in which God would write His law on human hearts and remember His people's sins no more (Jeremiah 31:31 to 34). Ezekiel described a new heart and a new spirit replacing hearts of stone (Ezekiel 36:26).

The Old Testament closes not with resolution but with anticipation.

## 9. The Story: Fulfillment and Consummation

After roughly four centuries of silence, the New Testament opens with the arrival of the deliverer promised in the garden. Paul explains the connection with precision, contrasting Adam, through whose one act of disobedience sin and death entered the world, with Christ, through whose one act of obedience many are made righteous, so that where sin abounded, grace abounded much more (Romans 5:12 to 21).

What Adam broke, Christ repairs. What the law could never fully accomplish, grace through Christ accomplishes completely. And this was not improvised. God chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world, predestining us for adoption as sons through Jesus Christ, in whom we have redemption through His blood (Ephesians 1:3 to 14).

The story does not end at the resurrection. It continues through the church's mission, and it has an ending. Revelation describes a new heaven and a new earth, where God Himself will dwell with His people, where He will wipe away every tear, and there shall be no more death, nor sorrow, nor crying, nor pain (Revelation 21:1 to 4).

:::quote Chapter Five
The garden that was lost in Genesis 3 becomes the city that is gained in Revelation 22, and the tree of life that humanity was barred from in Eden stands open once again, its leaves for the healing of the nations.
:::

## 10. Why This Framework Guards You

Jesus is not a character who shows up partway through the story to fix a problem that had gotten out of hand. He is the point of the story from the first promise in Genesis 3 to the last invitation in Revelation 22. This is why, when He explained the Scriptures after His resurrection, He treated the Old Testament as His own story, written centuries in advance (Luke 24:27, 44 to 47).

Smaller stories echo the larger one. Joseph, sold by his brothers and unjustly imprisoned, is raised up to save the very family that betrayed him, telling them they meant evil against him but God meant it for good (Genesis 50:20). The exodus tells of slaves delivered through the blood of a lamb, passing through water toward a promised inheritance. David is anointed king while another still sits on the throne, waiting years in obscurity before his kingdom is established.

Without this framework, believers fall into one of two errors: treating the Old Testament as a separate, almost unrelated collection of ancient stories, or dismissing it as obsolete. Both miss what Jesus insisted upon, that all of it was written concerning Him.

## Reflection

Take a single Old Testament narrative you know well. Write three sentences: one stating what happened, one stating what the passage is teaching about God, and one stating how it fits the storyline running toward Christ. Keep the three distinct. Most misreadings come from collapsing them.

## Summary

Scripture can be handled wrongly, so interpretation requires real effort and real skill. Context governs meaning, and the original audience must be heard before present application is attempted. Genre determines how a passage communicates, and ignoring it produces either wooden literalism or careless spiritualizing. Because one divine author stands behind the whole, clearer passages interpret less clear ones. Covenantal setting keeps us from both legalism and dismissal of the Old Testament. Method must be joined to the Spirit's illumination and to the accountability of the church rather than practiced in isolation. And every passage, rightly read in its own context, finally belongs to one story that runs from the promise in Genesis 3:15 through covenant, failure, exile, and anticipation to fulfillment in Christ and consummation in the new creation.

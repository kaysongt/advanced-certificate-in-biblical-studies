Leadership within the Church looks nothing like leadership as the world typically defines it, and Jesus made this distinction unmistakably clear to His own disciples.

In Mark 10, James and John had just asked for positions of honour in Christ's coming kingdom, a request that provoked indignation among the other ten. Jesus used the moment to correct a misunderstanding that has plagued religious leadership throughout history.

:::objectives By the end of this lesson you will be able to
1. Contrast worldly leadership with the servant model Jesus established in Mark 10.
2. Explain the shepherd image in Acts 20 and what the phrase *bought with His own blood* implies.
3. Explain the equipping purpose of leadership in Ephesians 4 and what it rules out.
4. State the character of the qualifications in 1 Timothy 3 and what they conspicuously omit.
5. Name the three temptations Peter identifies for leaders and the virtue countering each.
6. Explain the strategic blueprint of Acts 1:8 and the logic of Romans 10.
7. Distinguish evangelism from missions and explain the ambassador framework.
:::

## Key Terms

:::terms
Servant Leadership | | Leadership operating through service, sacrifice, and the laying down of personal advantage, as opposed to hierarchy, control, and accumulated status.
Overseer | Greek *episkopos* | One charged with watching over the flock. Used interchangeably with elder in the New Testament.
Shepherd | Greek *poimen* | The pastoral image whose authority exists entirely for the benefit of the sheep rather than the shepherd's advantage.
Equipping | Greek *katartismos* | The purpose for which gifted leaders were given: preparing God's people to do the work of ministry themselves.
Evangelism | | Proclamation of the gospel wherever a believer finds themselves, in neighbourhood, workplace, or family.
Missions | | The deliberate, often cross-cultural sending of believers into contexts where the gospel has not yet taken root.
Ambassador | Greek *presbeuo* | One who represents a sovereign authority and communicates that authority's message with accuracy and urgency.
:::

## 1. Not So With You

You know that those who are regarded as rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, Jesus said, and their high officials exercise authority over them. **Not so with you.** Whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant.

:::quote Chapter Nine
Worldly leadership operates through hierarchy, control, and the accumulation of status. Christ's leadership, and the leadership He calls His Church to embody, operates through service, sacrifice, and the deliberate laying down of personal advantage for the sake of others.
:::

Any model of church leadership that drifts toward domination, self-promotion, or the accumulation of personal power has departed from the pattern Jesus established, regardless of how much biblical language surrounds it.

## 2. Shepherds of a Purchased Flock

Paul reinforces the servant-shepherd model when he addresses the Ephesian elders. Keep watch over yourselves and all the flock of which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers. Be shepherds of the church of God, **which He bought with His own blood**.

The shepherd image carries specific implications. A shepherd's authority exists entirely for the benefit of the sheep, not for the shepherd's own advantage. A shepherd who exploits the flock for personal gain has fundamentally betrayed the purpose of the role.

Note also that final phrase. Church leaders are entrusted with the care of a people who belong, ultimately, to Christ, purchased at an infinite cost.

:::note What this should produce in a leader
A sober awareness that they are stewards of something immeasurably precious, not owners of a personal domain.
:::

## 3. Equipping, Not Performing

Ephesians 4 is explicit about the purpose of gifted leaders. It was to **equip His people for works of service**, so that the body of Christ may be built up until we all reach unity in the faith and become mature.

:::warn A crucial corrective
Leaders are not given so that they can perform ministry on behalf of a spectating congregation. They are given to equip the congregation to do the ministry themselves. A pastor who preaches excellent sermons every week but never trains and releases others to teach, counsel, evangelize, and serve has only partially fulfilled the calling Paul describes.
:::

Healthy leadership multiplies ministry capacity throughout the entire congregation rather than concentrating it in a single gifted individual. This requires humility, a willingness to release responsibility and authority to others, and patience, since equipping others takes considerably longer than simply doing the work oneself.

## 4. Qualifications That Are Strikingly Ordinary

First Timothy 3 lists qualifications for overseers with striking specificity: above reproach, faithful to his spouse, temperate, self-controlled, respectable, hospitable, able to teach, not given to drunkenness, not violent but gentle, not quarrelsome, not a lover of money, managing his own family well, not a recent convert, and having a good reputation with outsiders.

:::quote Chapter Nine
What stands out immediately about this list is how ordinary, even mundane, most of these qualifications are. Paul does not require exceptional charisma, dynamic public speaking ability, or impressive credentials. He requires character.
:::

This should reshape how congregations select and evaluate their leaders. Giftedness and charisma are not the primary qualifications for spiritual leadership. Proven, tested character, demonstrated over time in the ordinary relationships of family and community, matters more than platform ability.

The qualifications for deacons echo similar themes, and Paul insists that they too must **first be tested**. Testing implies time and observation. Leadership qualifications are not something a congregation can assess after a brief acquaintance.

## 5. Three Temptations, Three Virtues

Peter gives one of the New Testament's most direct instructions on the *manner* in which leadership should be exercised, naming three specific temptations and the virtue countering each.

| Temptation | Countering virtue |
|---|---|
| Reluctant obligation, serving because you must | Willing eagerness |
| Pursuit of dishonest gain | Eager service offered freely |
| Domineering control, lording it over those entrusted | Humble example |

That final phrase, **being examples to the flock**, deserves emphasis. Peter is describing leadership rooted in modelling rather than merely instructing. A shepherd leads sheep not primarily by commanding them verbally but by walking ahead of them, showing them the path through lived example.

Peter also gives a striking promise. When the Chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the crown of glory that will never fade away. This reframes the entire enterprise. It is not ultimately about building a personal platform or receiving recognition in this present life. It is about faithful stewardship of a flock that belongs to Christ, with the reward coming from the Chief Shepherd rather than from human applause.

This chapter also carries responsibility for congregations, not only leaders. Congregations bear responsibility to evaluate potential leaders according to the biblical qualifications rather than on the basis of charisma, wealth, education, or social status. And they bear responsibility to support and honour faithful leaders who genuinely shepherd according to the servant model.

## 6. The Scope of the Commission

Every believer carries a responsibility that cannot be delegated entirely to missionaries, evangelists, or paid ministry staff.

Mark's version of the commission records the scope plainly: go into all the world and preach the gospel to all creation. Total scope. No geographic or cultural boundary exempt.

Acts 1:8 gives the strategic blueprint. You will receive power when the Holy Spirit comes on you, and you will be My witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.

This verse describes both the **source** of evangelistic power and the **expanding scope** of responsibility, moving outward in concentric circles from the immediate local context to the farthest reaches of the world. The same pattern should shape how every congregation thinks about its own strategy, beginning with immediate neighbours and coworkers and extending outward.

:::quote Chapter Ten
Evangelism divorced from dependence on the Spirit becomes either a manipulative technique or an exhausting burden. Evangelism empowered by the Spirit flows from a heart genuinely transformed by the gospel.
:::

## 7. The Logic of Romans 10

Paul begins with a foundational statement, everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved, and then works backward through the logical steps required.

> How can they call on the one they have not believed in? → How can they believe in the one of whom they have not heard? → How can they hear without someone preaching to them? → How can anyone preach unless they are sent?

This establishes evangelism as an indispensable link, not because human words carry independent saving power, but because God has ordained that the gospel be communicated through human witnesses.

:::warn A common but mistaken assumption
This should dismantle the assumption that a person's evangelistic responsibility ends once they have lived a quietly exemplary life, allowing their actions to speak for themselves. A godly life adorns the gospel and removes unnecessary obstacles to belief, but it does not substitute for the actual proclamation of who Jesus is and what He has done. Someone, at some point, has to speak.
:::

## 8. Ambassadors

Second Corinthians introduces the framework that clarifies the theological basis. God has committed to us the message of reconciliation. We are therefore Christ's ambassadors, as though God were making His appeal through us.

An ambassador does not represent themselves or advocate for their own interests. They represent a sovereign authority and communicate that authority's message with accuracy and urgency.

:::quote Chapter Ten
When a believer shares the gospel with someone, they are not offering a personal opinion about religious preference. They are functioning as Christ's official representative, carrying His appeal to a world that desperately needs reconciliation with its Creator.
:::

This is both sobering and dignifying. Sobering because it demands faithfulness to the actual message rather than a diluted version. Dignifying because ordinary believers have been entrusted with the very voice of God's appeal.

It also addresses the hesitation many believers feel, a fear of rejection or awkwardness. An ambassador does not deliver their message apologetically, as though embarrassed by the content, nor aggressively, disregarding the dignity of the person receiving it. They deliver it with clarity, respect, and a genuine sense of urgency.

## 9. Evangelism and Missions Distinguished

| | Evangelism | Missions |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | Wherever a believer finds themselves: neighbourhood, workplace, family | Deliberate sending into contexts where the gospel has not taken root |
| **Boundaries** | Ordinary daily life | Often crossing geographic, linguistic, or cultural boundaries |
| **Calling** | Every believer | Not every believer, though every congregation must support and send |

A congregation that focuses exclusively on its own immediate community, however faithful that local ministry might be, has not fully grasped the scope of Christ's commission, which extends to the ends of the earth. Healthy congregations maintain both a local and a global vision.

Finally, sophistication is not required. The Samaritan woman, moments after her own encounter with Jesus, ran back to her town and told everyone, come, see a man who told me everything I ever did. Her testimony, simple and personal, brought many of her townspeople to faith. A genuine, personal account of what Christ has done carries real evangelistic power even without extensive theological training.

## Discussion Questions

1. Jesus said *not so with you*. Name a specific leadership practice you have observed in a church that would fail this test, and describe what the servant alternative would look like.
2. Paul's qualifications for elders omit charisma and platform ability entirely. How are leaders actually selected in the contexts you know, and what does the gap reveal?
3. Peter names three temptations and three countering virtues. Which temptation is most common among leaders you have known?
4. Trace the logic of Romans 10 backward. Where does the chain most often break in your congregation?
5. Explain the ambassador framework to someone who feels too unqualified to share their faith. What does it add, and what pressure does it remove?

## Reflection

Write down the name of one person in your ordinary life who does not yet know Christ. Then write one specific, realistic next step, not a plan to deliver a presentation, but a step toward the kind of relationship in which the conversation becomes natural.

## Summary

Jesus drew a hard line between worldly leadership and the leadership He requires: not hierarchy and status but service and sacrifice. Paul reinforces it with the shepherd image and the reminder that the flock was bought with Christ's own blood, which makes every leader a steward rather than an owner. Ephesians establishes that leaders exist to equip rather than to perform, which rules out any model where a congregation watches while a professional ministers. Paul's qualifications are conspicuously ordinary, requiring tested character rather than charisma, and requiring time to assess. Peter names three temptations and their countering virtues, and grounds a leader's reward in the Chief Shepherd rather than human applause. Turning to mission, the commission's scope is total, its power is the Spirit's, and Romans 10 shows why proclamation cannot be replaced by exemplary living alone. Every believer is an ambassador; not every believer is sent cross-culturally, though every congregation must send. Lesson 6 closes the course.

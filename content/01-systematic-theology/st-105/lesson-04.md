Jesus left His Church very few explicit instructions about ritual practice, but the two He did give carry enormous weight. These are not optional traditions congregations may adopt or discard according to preference. They are **ordinances**, meaning ordered practices, commanded directly by Christ and observed continuously by His people from the earliest days until now.

The same Great Commission that commands baptism also contains a word often overlooked despite carrying enormous weight. Jesus does not command His followers to make converts. He commands them to make **disciples**.

:::objectives By the end of this topic you will be able to
1. Explain why baptism is a normal expected step rather than an optional add-on.
2. State precisely what baptism does and does not accomplish.
3. Explain the theological meaning of baptism from Romans 6 and the three dimensions of its testimony.
4. Explain the Lord's Supper as remembrance, participation, and proclamation, including its forward orientation.
5. Explain Paul's warning about unworthy participation and its intended effect.
6. Explain why Christ gave physical, sensory practices rather than relying on verbal teaching alone.
7. Distinguish making converts from making disciples, and explain the goal stated in the Great Commission.
8. Explain the four-generation chain in 2 Timothy 2:2 and its implications for ordinary believers.
:::

## Key Terms

:::terms
Ordinance | | An ordered practice commanded directly by Christ. The Church observes two: baptism and the Lord's Supper.
Immersion | | The mode of baptism that most vividly displays the burial and resurrection imagery Paul describes in Romans 6.
Remembrance | Greek *anamnesis* | Not merely recalling facts but a participatory act engaging the senses, proclaiming the gospel through a shared meal.
Discerning the Body | | Paul's phrase in 1 Corinthians 11 for the self-examination required before approaching the Lord's Table.
Disciple | Greek *mathetes*, learner | Someone in a lifelong, ongoing process of learning to follow Jesus in every dimension of life, as distinct from a convert who has made a decision.
:::

## 1. Baptism as a Normal Step

The command comes directly from Jesus in the Great Commission. Go and make disciples of all nations, **baptizing** them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey everything I have commanded you.

Notice that baptism is woven directly into the process of making disciples. It is not presented as an optional add-on for especially devoted believers. It is a normal, expected step in becoming a follower of Jesus.

The pattern holds throughout Acts. At Pentecost, when the crowd asked what they should do, Peter answered directly: repent and be baptized, every one of you. Those who accepted his message were baptized, and about three thousand were added that day.

:::warn What baptism does not do
Baptism does not save a person, nor does it produce the forgiveness of sins through the water itself, as though the act carried some kind of magical power. Salvation comes through faith in Christ's finished work, not through any ritual, however sacred.
:::

What it *is*: the divinely appointed sign and public declaration of that salvation, the moment when an inward reality, faith and repentance, is expressed through an outward, visible act.

## 2. What Baptism Dramatizes

Paul explains the deeper meaning in Romans 6. Baptism is not merely a symbol of cleansing from sin, though it signifies that as well. It is a dramatic re-enactment of the gospel itself, death, burial, and resurrection, applied personally to the believer.

When a believer goes under the water, they are visibly proclaiming their identification with Christ's death, the old self being put to death with Him. When they are raised up, they are proclaiming their identification with Christ's resurrection, walking now in newness of life.

:::quote Chapter Seven
The imagery Paul describes, being buried and raised, points toward full immersion as the practice that most vividly displays the truth being proclaimed.
:::

Baptism testifies in three directions simultaneously.

| Direction | What it declares |
|---|---|
| To the believer | A definite, memorable point of identification with Christ |
| To the congregation and community | A public announcement that this person now belongs to Jesus |
| To the spiritual realm | A visible declaration of transfer from the domain of darkness into the kingdom of God's beloved Son |

This is why the Church has always treated baptism with seriousness, never reducing it to a private, casual, or optional matter.

## 3. The Lord's Supper

Paul recounts the tradition he received and passed on, tracing it directly back to the night Jesus was betrayed. Jesus took bread and said, this is My body, which is for you, do this in remembrance of Me. In the same way He took the cup, saying, this cup is the new covenant in My blood.

The command to do this in remembrance establishes an ongoing practice rather than a single historical event confined to the upper room.

:::quote Chapter Seven
This is not merely an intellectual memory exercise, recalling facts about a historical event. It is a participatory act, tasting bread and wine, engaging the senses in a way that words alone cannot accomplish, and proclaiming through that shared meal the central truth of the gospel.
:::

Paul then explains the proclamation. Whenever you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the Lord's death **until He comes**.

Notice the forward orientation. The Lord's Supper is not only a look backward toward the cross. It is also a look forward toward Christ's return. Every observance holds together the entire scope of the gospel story: His death accomplished, His presence known, and His return anticipated.

## 4. Approaching the Table

Paul warns the Corinthians against eating the bread or drinking the cup in an unworthy manner, without discerning the body of Christ, and connects careless participation to real spiritual and even physical consequences.

:::note What the warning is for
This warning is not meant to produce paralyzing fear that keeps believers from the table. It is meant to produce careful self-examination, a genuine assessment of one's own heart and relationships within the Body before approaching a meal that proclaims unity with Christ and with one another.
:::

## 5. Why Physical Signs

Both ordinances share a common structure. Each takes an ordinary physical element, water, bread, and wine, and invests it with profound spiritual meaning through Christ's command and the Church's obedient practice. Neither operates automatically or magically, and neither substitutes for genuine faith.

Why did Christ give His Church physical, sensory practices rather than relying entirely on verbal teaching?

:::quote Chapter Seven
Human beings are not purely intellectual creatures. We remember through ritual, through repeated physical action, through shared communal experience, in ways that abstract instruction alone rarely accomplishes. The whole gathered Body, regardless of background, education, or eloquence, participates equally in the same meal, proclaiming the same gospel with their hands and mouths, not merely their minds.
:::

## 6. Converts and Disciples

The Great Commission's main command is to make disciples. Three participial actions flow from it: going, baptizing, and teaching.

:::quote Chapter Eight
A convert is someone who has made a decision, while a disciple is someone who has entered a lifelong, ongoing process of learning to follow Jesus in every dimension of life.
:::

The final phrase deserves particular attention: **teaching them to obey** everything I have commanded you. Discipleship is not primarily an academic exercise focused on accumulating theological knowledge, though sound doctrine certainly matters. The goal is obedience, a transformed life.

A disciple who can recite doctrine flawlessly but whose daily life shows no evidence of transformation has missed the point of what Jesus commanded His Church to do.

Jesus describes the mark of genuine discipleship in John 8. If you hold to My teaching, you are really My disciples. Then you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free.

Note the conditional structure. Genuine discipleship is evidenced by *continuing* in Jesus' teaching over time. This is not a statement about earning salvation through obedience. It is a statement about the authenticity of professed faith.

The promise attached is significant. Freedom, in the biblical sense, is not primarily freedom from external restriction but freedom from the internal bondage of sin, ignorance, and self-deception. A disciple who continues learning and applying Jesus' teaching over years experiences an increasing liberation from patterns that once controlled them.

## 7. Rooted and Built Up

Paul describes the ongoing nature of discipleship in Colossians using two images at once. **Rooted** suggests organic growth, a plant drawing nourishment steadily from good soil over time. **Built up** suggests construction, a building rising course by course on a stable foundation.

Both communicate the same truth. Discipleship is not a single event but an ongoing, cumulative process.

:::warn Two errors this corrects
It is unrealistic, and ultimately discouraging, to expect dramatic, instantaneous transformation in every area of life the moment someone comes to faith. But growth also does not happen automatically simply because time passes. A plant left in poor soil without water does not grow, it withers. Discipleship requires deliberate investment: feeding on Scripture, engaging in prayer, participating in the life of the Church, and submitting to correction.
:::

## 8. The Four-Generation Chain

One of the most significant and often neglected dimensions of discipleship is the responsibility of more mature believers to invest in others. Paul makes it explicit: the things you have heard me say in the presence of many witnesses, entrust to reliable people who will also be qualified to teach others.

This single verse describes a chain spanning at least four generations.

> Paul → Timothy → reliable people → still others

Discipleship was never meant to terminate with an individual's own private growth. It was meant to reproduce.

This has practical implications many congregations have lost sight of. Discipleship is not a programme to be outsourced entirely to pastoral staff, nor is it accomplished merely by attending a weekly service. Paul's model assumes personal, relational investment: mature believers pouring their lives into less mature believers, teaching not only content but modelling character, handling conflict, exercising faith under pressure, and persevering through difficulty.

:::quote Chapter Eight
Reliable people, not necessarily formally trained theologians, are entrusted with passing on what they have received. Every believer who has walked with Christ for any length of time has something to offer someone newer in the faith. Discipleship is the normal responsibility of ordinary Christians, not a specialized function reserved for a professional few.
:::

Growth also requires honest engagement with correction. A disciple who never experiences correction, whether through the conviction of Scripture, the gentle rebuke of a mature believer, or the discipline of consequences, remains stagnant regardless of how much information they accumulate. Congregations that avoid any form of correction, prioritizing comfort over growth, tend to produce believers who remain perpetually immature however long they have attended.

Two questions close the chapter. Are you continuing in Jesus' teaching? And are you entrusting what you have learned to someone else?

Identify one believer newer in the faith than you. Write down one thing you have learned that they have not yet, and one concrete way you could pass it on this month.

## Summary

Christ gave His Church two ordinances, and both are commanded rather than optional. Baptism is woven into the making of disciples as a normal expected step, and while it saves no one, it is the appointed sign of a salvation received by faith. Romans 6 shows it re-enacting death, burial, and resurrection, and it testifies at once to the believer, to the watching community, and to the spiritual realm. The Lord's Supper is remembrance that is participatory rather than merely intellectual, and it proclaims the Lord's death until He comes, holding past, present, and future together. Paul's warning aims at self-examination rather than fear. Both ordinances take ordinary elements and invest them with meaning, because people remember through shared physical action in ways teaching alone cannot achieve. And the commission behind them commands disciples rather than converts, a process rooted and built up over time, reproducing through ordinary believers across at least four generations.

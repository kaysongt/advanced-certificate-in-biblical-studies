This lesson holds together the two halves of the mystery. Neither can be surrendered, and the church has spent centuries learning that softening either one collapses the gospel.

Dr. Kay names a temptation that runs particularly among believers who rightly cherish the deity of Christ.

:::quote Chapter Three
It can feel safer, somehow, to imagine Jesus moving through His earthly life with a kind of divine immunity to the discomforts the rest of us endure. But Scripture does not describe a Savior who merely appeared human while secretly floating above the difficulties of life on earth. It describes a Man who grew, who tired, who wept, who bled, and who was tempted in every way that we are.
:::

:::objectives By the end of this lesson you will be able to
1. Demonstrate the genuine humanity of Christ from the Gospels' record of His growth, weariness, grief, hunger, thirst, and sleep.
2. Explain Hebrews 4:15 to 16 and why the reality of Christ's temptation is the ground of the believer's confidence.
3. Explain why Christ's sinlessness was essential to redemption, using Romans 5:19.
4. Establish the deity of Christ from four categories of evidence: names, attributes, works, and worship received.
5. Explain why the deity of Christ cannot be softened without collapsing the gospel.
6. State the doctrine of the two natures within its orthodox boundaries and identify the two characteristic errors.
:::

## Key Terms

:::terms
Hypostatic Union | | The union of the divine and human natures in the one Person of Christ, without confusion and without separation.
Two Natures | | Christ possesses two complete and undiminished natures, divine and human, in one Person. He is not two Persons, and the natures do not blend into a third hybrid thing.
Impeccability | | The sinlessness of Christ. Not merely that He did not sin, but that His obedience was complete across a genuinely tempted human life.
Docetism | Greek *dokeo*, to seem | The early error teaching that Jesus only appeared to be human or only appeared to suffer. John writes against it directly in 1 John 4:2 to 3.
Substitution | | Christ standing in the place of sinners, His obedience credited to those who trust Him, as Paul sets out in Romans 5:19.
Mediator | | One who stands between two parties. Christ mediates between God and man, which requires that He be genuinely both.
:::

## 1. A Man Who Grew

Luke tells us plainly that as a boy, Jesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in favour with God and men (Luke 2:52). That word *increased* matters. Jesus did not arrive at the manger already possessing the full range of human knowledge and experience He would carry as an adult. He learned to walk. He learned to speak Aramaic. He likely learned His earthly father's trade as a carpenter. He grew the way every human child grows, one season at a time.

## 2. Tired, Grieving, Hungry, Asleep

The Gospels do not hide the ordinary textures of His humanity.

John records a scene at a well in Samaria where Jesus, being wearied from His journey, sat thus by the well (John 4:6). He was tired. His body needed rest the same way yours does.

At the tomb of His friend Lazarus we read the shortest and perhaps most moving verse in Scripture: Jesus wept (John 11:35). He was not performing grief for the crowd. He genuinely felt the weight of loss, even though He knew He was about to raise Lazarus from the dead moments later.

He hungered after fasting in the wilderness (Matthew 4:2). He experienced thirst on the cross (John 19:28). He needed sleep, so much so that He was found asleep in the back of a boat during a storm that terrified seasoned fishermen (Mark 4:38).

:::quote Chapter Three
Every one of these details is a small, deliberate brushstroke in the larger portrait Scripture is painting for us. Jesus was not merely disguised as a man. He was a man, in every sense that matters, body and soul.
:::

## 3. Tempted as We Are

Here the humanity of Christ becomes not a biographical detail but a source of profound comfort. We do not have a High Priest who cannot sympathize with our weaknesses, but was in all points tempted as we are, yet without sin (Hebrews 4:15).

Think carefully about what this verse claims. Jesus was tempted in every point that we are. Hunger, fatigue, the desire for comfort, the pull toward pride, the pressure to compromise under threat. He faced it all, and He never once gave in.

He is not a distant deity who issues commands from a safe and untouched vantage point, unable to understand why obedience feels so costly. He is a High Priest who has walked the same terrain we walk. Hebrews draws the conclusion a few verses later, urging us to come boldly to the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to help in time of need (Hebrews 4:16).

**The wilderness.** The temptation came not after a season of comfort but after forty days of fasting, precisely when human resistance is weakest (Matthew 4:1 to 2). Each temptation targeted a genuine human vulnerability: the desire to satisfy physical hunger through a shortcut, the desire for a spectacular demonstration of divine favour, the desire for worldly power without the cost of the cross. Jesus met each one not by drawing on a hidden reserve of divine strength unavailable to the rest of us, but by standing on the authority of Scripture, answering each time from Deuteronomy.

**Gethsemane.** Mark tells us Jesus began to be troubled and deeply distressed, telling His closest disciples that His soul was exceedingly sorrowful, even to death (Mark 14:33 to 34). Luke adds that His sweat became like great drops of blood falling to the ground (Luke 22:44). This was a genuine human soul, wrestling honestly with the horror of what lay ahead, and arriving through prayer at the settled resolve to submit fully: not My will, but Yours, be done (Luke 22:42).

:::quote Chapter Three
His humanity did not make His obedience easier. It made His obedience costly, deliberate, and real, which is precisely why it carries such weight on our behalf.
:::

## 4. Why His Sinlessness Was Essential

Paul explains the mechanism in Romans, contrasting Adam and Christ: for as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so also by one Man's obedience many will be made righteous (Romans 5:19).

Adam's disobedience plunged the human race into sin. Christ's obedience, lived out across a genuinely human life, provided the righteousness that could be credited to those who trust in Him.

This is why even the smallest lapse would have disqualified Him. If Jesus had sinned even once, He would have needed a Savior of His own, and He could not have served as a substitute for anyone else.

:::warn Why the suffering cannot be softened
Some ancient errors suggested that Jesus only appeared to suffer, that His divine nature shielded Him from the full weight of physical pain, or that a substitute secretly took His place on the cross. John wrote forcefully against teaching of this kind, insisting that Jesus Christ came in the flesh, and that anyone denying this was not speaking by the Spirit of God (1 John 4:2 to 3). A Savior who did not genuinely suffer could not genuinely bear our sin in His body, which is precisely what Peter says He did (1 Peter 2:24).
:::

## 5. The Deity of Christ: Four Lines of Evidence

Jesus Christ is not merely a Man who was especially close to God, or a prophet uniquely favoured by heaven. He is God, in the fullest and most unqualified sense, and the Bible presses this claim from four directions at once.

**The names He carries.** John's Gospel opens with the direct statement that the Word was God (John 1:1), a claim so unambiguous that translators through the centuries have wrestled to soften it without success, because the Greek grammar will not permit a lesser reading. When religious leaders pressed Jesus about His identity, He answered with a phrase deliberately echoing the burning bush: most assuredly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I AM (John 8:58). His listeners understood exactly what He was claiming, which is why they immediately picked up stones. Thomas, seeing the risen Christ, declared, my Lord and my God (John 20:28), and Jesus did not correct him. Paul describes believers as looking for the glorious appearing of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ (Titus 2:13). Hebrews records the Father Himself addressing the Son: Your throne, O God, is forever and ever (Hebrews 1:8).

**The attributes He possesses.** In Him dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily (Colossians 2:9). Not a partial reflection, not a diluted version, but the fullness. He displayed omniscience, knowing the thoughts of those around Him (Matthew 9:4) and knowing beforehand who would betray Him (John 6:64). He displayed omnipotence, calming storms with a word (Mark 4:39) and raising the dead by His command (John 11:43).

**The works He performs.** Certain works are reserved exclusively for God. Creation is one, and all things were created through Christ (Colossians 1:16). The forgiveness of sins is another. When Jesus told a paralysed man his sins were forgiven, the religious leaders understood immediately, asking, who can forgive sins but God alone (Mark 2:7). Jesus did not deny the implication. He healed the man to prove His authority was genuine (Mark 2:10 to 11). Sustaining the universe is a divine prerogative, and Hebrews attributes it to Him (Hebrews 1:3). Judgment is reserved for God, and yet the Father has committed all judgment to the Son (John 5:22).

**The worship He receives.** This may be the clearest test of all. When John, overwhelmed by a vision, fell down to worship an angel, the angel stopped him: see that you do not do that. Worship God (Revelation 22:9). Peter refused worship when Cornelius bowed before him (Acts 10:26). Angels refuse worship. Faithful men refuse worship. Jesus received it repeatedly, from the wise men at His birth, from His disciples after He calmed the storm, from the formerly blind man He healed, and from Thomas, and in every case He accepted it without correction.

:::quote Chapter Four
A created being who accepted worship would be guilty of the gravest sin in Scripture. Jesus accepted it because He is not a created being. He is God.
:::

## 6. Why This Doctrine Cannot Be Softened

Some have tried to describe Jesus as a great teacher, a moral exemplar, or an especially anointed prophet while stopping short of confessing His full deity. That middle position collapses under the weight of His own claims. As C. S. Lewis observed, a man who said the things Jesus said would not be left open to us as merely a great moral teacher.

The deeper reason is that salvation depends on it.

:::quote Chapter Four
Only God possesses the infinite worth necessary to atone for sin committed against an infinitely holy God. A finite sacrifice, however good or however sincere, could never satisfy an infinite debt.
:::

## 7. One Person, Two Natures

Holding both chapters together raises the question the church has wrestled with since its earliest centuries. How can one Person be fully God and fully man at the same time, without either nature being diminished or the two blending into some third, hybrid thing?

Scripture does not offer a tidy philosophical formula. It does give us the boundaries within which the mystery must be held.

> Jesus is **one Person**, not two, and He possesses **two complete and undiminished natures**, divine and human, **united without confusion and without separation**.

This is why the Gospels can describe Him sleeping in a boat, exhausted like any man, and calming the storm with a word, acting with the authority of God, within the very same narrative. He was not switching between two modes. He was, at every moment, fully both, one Person acting through two natures in perfect harmony.

| Error | What it does |
|---|---|
| Overemphasizing deity | Makes His humanity seem like a costume |
| Overemphasizing humanity | Ends in questioning His full deity |

:::note The safest posture
Church history offers plenty of examples of well-meaning teachers who tried to simplify this in ways that distorted one truth or the other. The safest posture is the posture of worship rather than explanation, believing what Scripture plainly teaches even where it exceeds our ability to fully diagram it.
:::

## 8. Why You Need Both

Consider what it means, practically, that the One who intercedes for you in heaven right now is fully God and fully man at once.

Because He is **fully man**, He understands your weakness from the inside. Because He is **fully God**, His intercession carries infinite authority and cannot fail.

A merely human mediator could sympathize with you but could not save you. A mediator who was God alone, without ever having lived a genuinely human life, could save you but might not fully understand the texture of your struggle. In Christ you have both at once, a Savior whose sympathy is real and whose power is absolute.

## 9. The Same Yesterday, Today, and Forever

Hebrews closes a long argument about the greatness of Christ's priesthood with a sweeping declaration: Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever (Hebrews 13:8).

This is not a claim any created being could honestly make. Every human life is marked by change, growth, decline, and eventual death. Every earthly kingdom rises and falls. Every fashionable idea of one generation is quietly abandoned by the next. The Christ who walked the shores of Galilee is the same Christ reigning at the Father's right hand today, and He will be the same Christ believers meet face to face at the end of the age.

:::quote Chapter Four
This unchanging nature is not cold or static, the way a stone monument remains unchanged simply because it is lifeless. It is the settled, living constancy of a Person whose perfection leaves no room for improvement and whose faithfulness leaves no room for failure.
:::

## Summary

Jesus grew, tired, wept, hungered, thirsted, and slept, and Scripture records these details deliberately. He was tempted in every point as we are, in the wilderness and in Gethsemane, and He resisted by standing on Scripture rather than by drawing on power unavailable to us. That reality is the ground of our confidence to come boldly to the throne of grace. His sinlessness was essential, since one lapse would have disqualified Him as a substitute. At the same time, four independent lines of evidence establish His full deity: the divine names He carries, the divine attributes He possesses, the divine works He performs, and the worship He receives without correction. The doctrine cannot be softened, because only God possesses the infinite worth to atone for sin against God. Held together, He is one Person in two complete natures, without confusion and without separation, and He is exactly the mediator that weak and weary people need. Lesson 4 turns from who He is to what He came to do.

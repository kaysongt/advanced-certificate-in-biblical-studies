There is a mistake many people make without realizing it. They think of Jesus as someone who came into existence the night He was born in Bethlehem. They picture His story starting with a manger, shepherds, and a star.

The Bible refuses to let us begin the story there. It insists on pulling the curtain back much further, into eternity itself, long before the first grain of dust was shaped into a mountain or the first drop of water filled the sea.

:::objectives By the end of this lesson you will be able to
1. Show from John, Colossians, and Hebrews that the Son existed eternally and is the agent of creation.
2. Explain the meaning of *firstborn over all creation* in Colossians 1:15 and refute the misreading of it.
3. Explain what John 17:5 reveals about the glory the Son shared with the Father before the world was.
4. Identify Old Testament anticipations of the eternal Son, including Micah 5:2 and Daniel 7:13 to 14.
5. State why the preexistence of Christ is necessary for salvation rather than an optional refinement.
:::

## Key Scriptures

:::scripture John 1:1
In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.
:::

Notice what John is doing. He is not introducing a new character. He is identifying someone who was already there when the beginning began. His opening echoes Genesis on purpose.

- **John 1:1, 1:3** — the Word was God, and all things were made through Him
- **Colossians 1:15 to 17** — the image of the invisible God, before all things
- **Hebrews 1:2 to 3** — upholding all things by the word of His power
- **John 17:5** — the glory which I had with You before the world was
- **John 10:30** — I and My Father are one
- **Micah 5:2** — His goings forth are from of old, from everlasting
- **Daniel 7:13 to 14** — one like the Son of Man receiving an everlasting kingdom
- **John 1:32 to 33** — the Spirit descending and remaining upon Him

## Key Terms

:::terms
Preexistence | | The truth that the Son existed as a divine Person before His incarnation, and indeed before creation itself.
Firstborn | Greek *prototokos* | In Jewish usage, a term of preeminence and rank rather than chronological origin. It does not mean first created.
Agent of Creation | | The scriptural teaching that all things were made through the Son, so that He stands on the Creator's side of the Creator and creature distinction.
Providence | | God's continuous sustaining of what He has made. Hebrews attributes this to the Son, who upholds all things by the word of His power.
Angel of the Lord | | A figure appearing in the Old Testament who speaks with the authority of God, accepts worship, and is identified with the Lord Himself. Many readers from the early church onward have recognized these as appearances of the preincarnate Son.
Son of Man | | The title Jesus applies to Himself more than any other in the Gospels, drawing His hearers back to Daniel 7 and claiming its fulfillment.
:::

## 1. The Word Who Was Already There

John continues in verse three with a statement that closes every loophole a skeptic might try to find. All things were made through Him, and without Him nothing was made that was made (John 1:3).

Every galaxy, every atom, every living creature came into being through Him. He did not evolve into deity. He did not earn His way into the Godhead through obedience or achievement. He has always been God, and He has always been the agent through whom God creates.

## 2. Paul and the Word *Firstborn*

Paul makes the same claim to the Colossians, describing Christ as the one who is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn over all creation, and adding that by Him all things were created that are in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, whether thrones or dominions or principalities or powers, and that all things were created through Him and for Him (Colossians 1:15 to 16).

The phrase *firstborn over all creation* has been misread throughout church history as though it made Christ the first created being. It does not. In Jewish usage the term carried the idea of preeminence and rank rather than chronological origin.

:::warn The decisive next verse
Paul removes any ambiguity immediately: He is before all things, and in Him all things consist (Colossians 1:17). You cannot be before all things and also be one of the things that was made.
:::

## 3. Still Upholding

Hebrews describes the Son as the one through whom God made the worlds, who is the brightness of His glory and the express image of His person, upholding all things by the word of His power (Hebrews 1:2 to 3).

That word *upholding* rewards attention. Christ is not the One who flipped the switch at creation and then stepped back to watch things run on their own. He is actively sustaining the universe at this very moment. Every law of physics, every heartbeat, every sunrise depends on His ongoing word of power.

There is a comfort tucked inside this that is easy to miss.

:::quote Chapter One
If Christ upholds all things by the word of His power, then nothing in your life is happening outside His sustaining care. The same power that keeps planets in orbit and holds atoms together is the power at work in the details of your ordinary days.
:::

## 4. A Glory Shared Before Time

The most personal window into Christ's eternal existence comes from His own lips. On the night before His crucifixion, Jesus prayed, and now, O Father, glorify Me together with Yourself, with the glory which I had with You before the world was (John 17:5).

Read that carefully. Jesus is not asking for a glory He has never known. He is asking to be restored to a glory He willingly set aside, a glory He shared with the Father in eternity past, before there was a world, before there was time as we understand it.

This is why the Trinity matters so deeply when we talk about Christ. He has never been alone. From eternity, the Son has existed in perfect, unbroken fellowship with the Father and the Spirit. When Jesus later says, I and My Father are one (John 10:30), He is not describing a relationship that began at His birth. He is describing a relationship that has always been.

## 5. Promised Before He Arrived

The Old Testament does not use the language of incarnation, and it still points forward to this eternal Person.

The prophet Micah, writing centuries before Bethlehem, spoke of a ruler who would come from that small town, and then added a phrase that startled generations of readers. This ruler's goings forth are from of old, from everlasting (Micah 5:2).

A child born in time, yet a Person whose existence stretches back into everlasting. Micah could not fully explain the mystery he was describing. He was inspired to point at it anyway.

## 6. The Son Threaded Through the Old Testament

Once you understand that the Son existed before Bethlehem, certain Old Testament passages take on new depth.

Several times in Genesis and the historical books, a figure called the Angel of the Lord appears, speaking with the authority of God Himself, accepting worship, and identifying so closely with God that the text moves seamlessly between calling Him the Angel of the Lord and simply calling Him the Lord. Many careful readers of Scripture, from the early church onward, have recognized these appearances as glimpses of the preincarnate Son.

Consider also Daniel's vision of one like the Son of Man, coming with the clouds of heaven, approaching the Ancient of Days, and receiving dominion, glory, and a kingdom that will never pass away (Daniel 7:13 to 14). Jesus later applies this very title to Himself more than any other title in the Gospels, deliberately drawing His listeners back to Daniel's vision and claiming its fulfillment as His own.

:::quote Chapter One
The Old Testament, read carefully and read whole, is not silent about the eternal Son. It is threaded through with His presence, waiting for the fullness of time when the veil would finally be lifted and He would step forward, not in vision or shadow, but in flesh and blood.
:::

## 7. Why This Chapter Comes First

You might wonder why a chapter on Christ's eternal preexistence deserves this much attention before we even reach the manger. Here is why.

If Jesus began His existence in Bethlehem, then He is a created being, however exalted. And if He is a created being, He cannot save us, because only God can atone for sin against God.

:::quote Chapter One
The entire structure of the gospel rests on the truth that the One who died for us is the same One who made us. He is not a middleman. He is not an angel promoted to a higher rank. He is God the Son, eternally existing, who stepped into the world He had already made.
:::

This truth should produce worship rather than merely information. Before Abraham was born, before Adam took his first breath, before the earth was formed, He was already there, already glorious, already loving you in the eternal counsel of God. That is where the story of Jesus really begins.

## Discussion Questions

1. Trace the argument from Colossians 1:15 to 17 as though you were answering someone who believes *firstborn* means Christ was created. What does verse 17 settle, and why?
2. John 17:5 shows Christ asking to be restored to a glory He had set aside. What does the word *restored* imply about the incarnation that we will take up in Lesson 2?
3. Hebrews says Christ upholds all things right now. Name one area of your life where you have been functioning as though God set the world running and stepped back.
4. Why does the salvation of sinners depend on the preexistence of the Son? Build the argument in three steps.
5. Read Daniel 7:13 to 14 alongside a Gospel passage where Jesus calls Himself the Son of Man. What was He claiming, and would His hearers have understood it?

## Summary

The story of Jesus does not begin at Bethlehem. John identifies the Word as already existing when the beginning began, fully God, and the agent through whom everything was made. Paul calls Him firstborn over all creation in the sense of rank rather than origin, and settles the matter by declaring Him before all things. Hebrews adds that He sustains the universe at this moment by His word. Jesus Himself prayed to be restored to the glory He shared with the Father before the world was, which presupposes an eternity of fellowship rather than a relationship beginning at His birth. Micah and Daniel pointed toward this Person centuries in advance. And all of it matters for one reason above the rest: only God can atone for sin against God, so if the Son began in Bethlehem, He could not save anyone. Lesson 2 asks what happened when this eternal Person took on flesh.

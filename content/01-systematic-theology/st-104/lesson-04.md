Repentance is one of the most misunderstood words in the Christian vocabulary. For many people it conjures images of guilt, grovelling, or emotional breakdowns meant to prove sincerity to God. That picture captures a fragment of the truth and misses the heart of what the Bible actually means.

This lesson pairs repentance with justification because they answer two halves of one question: what does a person do, and what does God declare.

:::objectives By the end of this lesson you will be able to
1. Define repentance from its Greek root and explain its relationship to faith.
2. Distinguish godly sorrow from worldly sorrow and identify the fruit each produces.
3. Explain why repentance is described in Scripture as a gift rather than a precondition we meet.
4. Define justification as a legal declaration and distinguish it from sanctification.
5. Explain how God remains just while justifying the ungodly.
6. Explain the great exchange and the imputation of Christ's righteousness.
7. Diagnose the performance pattern that persists among believers who affirm justification intellectually.
:::

## Key Terms

:::terms
Repentance | Greek *metanoia*, a change of mind | A fundamental reorientation of thinking that produces a corresponding change in direction and behaviour.
Godly Sorrow | | Grief that sin offended a holy and loving God, producing genuine turning and lasting fruit.
Worldly Sorrow | | Regret over consequences, focused on the self and its losses, producing nothing lasting.
Justification | | The legal declaration that a believer stands righteous before God, rendered once and completely rather than achieved gradually.
Propitiation | | A sacrifice that satisfies wrath and turns it away.
Imputation | | The crediting of Christ's righteousness to the believer's account, as Abraham's faith was credited to him.
The Great Exchange | | Christ treated as though He bore our sin, and believers treated as though they possess His righteousness (2 Corinthians 5:21).
:::

## 1. What Repentance Actually Is

The Greek word most often translated repentance in the New Testament, *metanoia*, literally means a change of mind. It describes a fundamental reorientation of thinking that produces a corresponding change in direction and behaviour.

:::quote Chapter Seven
Repentance is not primarily an emotional experience, though genuine repentance often does stir deep emotion. It is a decisive turn, a person walking one direction who stops, changes their mind about where they are headed, and begins walking the opposite way.
:::

Jesus began His public ministry with this exact call. The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand. Repent, and believe in the gospel (Mark 1:15).

Notice that repentance and faith are presented together, almost as two sides of a single coin. Repentance is turning away from confidence in self, sin, and the old way of life. Faith is turning toward confidence in Christ. You cannot have one without the other, because they describe the same turn viewed from two different angles.

## 2. Not Only Subtraction

Peter's sermon at Pentecost offers one of the clearest examples of repentance in action. When the crowd cries out asking what they should do, Peter answers: repent, and let every one of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and you shall receive the gift of the Holy Spirit.

Peter returns to the theme in Acts 3:19, urging his listeners to repent and be converted, that their sins might be blotted out, so that **times of refreshing** may come from the presence of the Lord.

:::note Repentance framed positively
Repentance is often framed only in negative terms, as though it were merely the subtraction of sin from a person's life. Scripture frames it just as much in positive terms, as the doorway into refreshment, renewal, and the presence of the Lord. Turning away from sin is not primarily about loss. It is about gain, trading something destructive for something life-giving.
:::

## 3. Two Kinds of Sorrow

Paul offers the most theologically precise treatment of repentance in the New Testament by contrasting two very different responses to conviction. Godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted, but the sorrow of the world produces death (2 Corinthians 7:10).

| | Worldly Sorrow | Godly Sorrow |
|---|---|---|
| **Grieves** | That sin was discovered, that reputation suffered, that punishment followed | That sin offended a holy and loving God |
| **Focus** | The self and its losses | God and the relationship damaged |
| **Fruit** | Fades as soon as the crisis passes; the same patterns reassert themselves | A genuinely altered trajectory, because it addresses the root |
| **Verdict** | Produces death | Not to be regretted |

Dr. Kay notes that he has watched both play out in the lives of people he has pastored. Worldly sorrow can be intense, even overwhelming, yet it produces nothing lasting because its focus remains entirely on the self.

## 4. The Father Who Runs

Jesus told a story that illustrates the true shape of repentance more vividly than any definition could. A younger son demands his inheritance early, leaves home, and squanders everything, until a famine leaves him feeding pigs.

Luke 15:17 describes the turning point with a simple phrase: **he came to himself**. That moment of clarity, seeing his situation honestly for the first time, is where repentance begins. He rehearses a speech, resolves to return home, and expects at best to be received as a hired servant. But the father, watching for him, sees him while he is still a great way off, runs to him, embraces him, and restores him immediately to full sonship.

:::quote Chapter Seven
This is the heart behind every call to repentance in Scripture. It is never God reluctantly readmitting a suspicious returnee on probation. It is a Father running to meet anyone willing to turn around and come home.
:::

Two further clarifications matter. First, repentance is not something we manufacture through sheer determination in order to earn God's forgiveness. Scripture describes repentance itself as a gift, something God grants. The same grace that saves us is the grace that turns us. Second, repentance is not a one-time event confined to conversion, though it begins there. It becomes the ongoing posture of a believer's life.

## 5. The Courtroom

Imagine standing in a courtroom, guilty of every charge, with evidence so overwhelming that no honest defence could ever be mounted. Then imagine the judge, instead of pronouncing the sentence your guilt deserves, declaring you not guilty, not because the evidence was wrong, but because Someone else has already served your sentence in full.

Justification is a legal term borrowed from the courtroom. To be justified means to be **declared** righteous, not gradually **made** righteous through a process.

:::warn The distinction that governs the whole course
Justification is not about becoming. It is about being declared, once and finally, on the basis of Christ's finished work. Confusing it with sanctification, the lifelong process of growing in Christlikeness, is the root of nearly every pastoral problem addressed in Lesson 6.
:::

Paul builds his case methodically, and by Romans 3:21 the argument reaches its turning point. Now the righteousness of God **apart from the law** is revealed, through faith in Jesus Christ, to all and on all who believe.

## 6. Just and the Justifier

Romans 3:24 to 28 unpacks how this righteousness becomes available. Believers are justified freely by His grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus, whom God set forth as a propitiation by His blood, through faith.

Propitiation describes a sacrifice that satisfies wrath and turns it away. Jesus did not merely demonstrate God's love on the cross. He satisfied the righteous demands of God's justice, absorbing the penalty sin deserved so that God could remain **both just and the justifier** of everyone who believes.

This answers an objection worth taking seriously.

:::quote Chapter Eight
In a human court, a judge who declares a guilty person innocent without dealing with the crime is corrupt, and the justice system itself is undermined. God is not declaring sinners righteous by ignoring the demands of justice. He is declaring them righteous because those demands have already been fully satisfied at the cross. It is not a legal loophole. It is the most costly and carefully accomplished verdict in the history of the universe.
:::

Paul concludes the section with a statement that eliminates any remaining trace of human boasting. Where is boasting then? It is excluded.

## 7. Peace as Legal Reality

Romans 5:1 and 2 describes the practical fruit. Having been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom also we have access by faith into this grace in which we stand.

Peace with God is not a feeling we work ourselves into through emotional effort. It is a legal reality that flows directly from justification. If God has declared you righteous, the war between you and Him is over, not because the charges were dropped for lack of evidence, but because they were fully paid.

This peace is not merely the absence of anxious feelings, though it often produces that as well. It is the ending of hostility between two parties formerly at war. Justification does not merely improve the relationship. It ends the hostility altogether, replacing it with **access**, the kind of open, welcomed access a child has to a loving parent rather than the guarded access a stranger might be granted.

## 8. The Great Exchange

Paul returns to this with concentrated force in 2 Corinthians 5:21. For He made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in Him.

Jesus, who never sinned, was treated as though He bore the full weight of our sin. In exchange, we, who have never been righteous on our own, are treated as though we possess the perfect righteousness of Christ Himself. This is not a righteousness we produce or maintain. It is credited to us, imputed to our account, the same way Abraham's faith was credited to him centuries before Christ came.

Paul's own testimony gives this a personal edge. Writing about his impressive religious credentials, including flawless observance of the law from a Pharisaic perspective, Paul describes counting all of it as loss. His desire is to be found in Him, not having my own righteousness, which is from the law, but that which is through faith in Christ (Philippians 3:9).

Paul, of all people, someone with more reason than most to trust his own religious performance, categorically rejects that performance as the ground of his standing before God.

## 9. Why the Doctrine Fails to Land

Dr. Kay names a pattern honestly.

:::quote Chapter Eight
I have met so many sincere believers who intellectually agree with justification by faith while continuing to live as though their standing with God rises and falls with their daily performance. If you had a good quiet time, you feel close to God. If you failed morally, you feel distant, as though your access to Him has been temporarily suspended pending better behavior. This is not the gospel.
:::

Justification means your standing before God does not fluctuate with your performance, because it was never based on your performance in the first place. That verdict does not need to be re-earned tomorrow morning.

This does not mean daily obedience does not matter. Lesson 6 will explain why it matters enormously. But obedience flows **from** a settled legal status, not **toward** one that remains perpetually uncertain.

## Discussion Questions

1. Explain why repentance and faith are two sides of one turn rather than two separate steps. What goes wrong when they are separated?
2. Work through the table in section 3. Describe someone you have known whose sorrow was worldly. What was the tell?
3. Repentance is described in Scripture as a gift God grants. How does that protect it from becoming a work we perform to qualify for grace?
4. Explain to a sceptic how God can declare a guilty person righteous without becoming a corrupt judge.
5. Dr. Kay's diagnosis in section 9 describes many believers. Where do you recognize the pattern in yourself, and what specifically would change if the verdict were settled in your practical experience?

## Reflection

Track your sense of closeness to God for three days. Each time it rises or falls, write down what changed. Then look at the list and ask honestly whether the causes are performance-based. Bring the list to the next session.

## Summary

Repentance is a change of mind that produces a change of direction, inseparable from faith and framed by Scripture as a doorway into refreshment rather than merely the subtraction of sin. Paul distinguishes godly sorrow, which grieves the offence against God and produces lasting change, from worldly sorrow, which grieves consequences and fades. Repentance is itself a gift, and it becomes the ongoing posture of a believer's life rather than a single event. Justification is the verdict that meets it: a legal declaration rendered once and completely on the basis of Christ's finished work, never a gradual process. God remains just while justifying because the penalty was paid rather than waived, which makes the verdict costly rather than a loophole. The result is peace as legal reality and access as a child rather than a stranger, grounded in a righteousness credited rather than produced. And the standing this establishes does not need re-earning each morning. Lesson 5 turns to what God then begins to do within us.

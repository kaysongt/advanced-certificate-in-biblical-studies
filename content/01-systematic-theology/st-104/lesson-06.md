Every doctrine in Lesson 5 describes something God accomplishes instantly and completely at the moment of salvation. Regeneration happens in a moment. Adoption is settled immediately and permanently. Union with Christ is an established reality the instant a person believes.

Sanctification is different, and understanding that difference will protect you from a great deal of confusion and discouragement.

:::objectives By the end of this lesson you will be able to
1. Define sanctification and distinguish it clearly from justification.
2. Explain the significance of the word *therefore* opening Romans 12.
3. Explain the renewal of the mind and the principle of beholding in 2 Corinthians 3:18.
4. Hold together divine work and human effort using Philippians 2:12 to 13, and name the two ditches.
5. Explain why sanctification requires community.
6. Define glorification and explain the significance of Paul's past tense in Romans 8:30.
7. Distinguish the objective from the subjective ground of assurance.
:::

## Key Terms

:::terms
Sanctification | | The lifelong process by which the Spirit conforms a believer's thoughts, desires, and behaviour increasingly into the likeness of Christ.
Positional vs Progressive | | Justification establishes a status once and completely; sanctification unfolds gradually and unevenly over a lifetime.
Glorification | | The final, instantaneous transformation at Christ's return, when believers receive resurrected bodies no longer subject to sin, sickness, aging, or death.
The Golden Chain | | Romans 8:29 to 30, linking foreknowledge, predestination, calling, justification, and glorification so tightly that Paul speaks of the last as already accomplished.
Objective Ground of Assurance | | The finished, unchanging work of Christ, true whether or not a believer feels it on any given day.
Subjective Ground of Assurance | | The inward witness of the Spirit and the observable fruit of a changed life, which can fluctuate.
:::

## 1. The Ordering of Romans 12

Paul addresses sanctification directly in Romans 12:1 and 2, opening the most practical section of his letter. Present your bodies a living sacrifice. Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind.

Notice the word **therefore** at the opening. Paul has spent eleven chapters describing the mercies of God: grace, justification, adoption, and the security of the believer's new identity. Only after establishing that foundation does he turn to practical implications.

:::quote Chapter Twelve
This ordering matters enormously. Obedience in the Christian life is always a response to grace already received, never a strategy for earning grace not yet given.
:::

## 2. How Transformation Actually Happens

The renewing of the mind is central to how sanctification works. Transformation does not occur primarily through willpower or gritted determination, though effort certainly plays a genuine role. It occurs through a mind increasingly saturated with truth, increasingly reoriented away from the patterns of thinking the world constantly presses upon us.

As thinking changes, behaviour gradually follows, because our actions flow from what we genuinely believe to be true, valuable, and desirable.

Paul offers a complementary image in 2 Corinthians 3:18. We all, with unveiled face, **beholding** as in a mirror the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from glory to glory.

:::quote Chapter Twelve
Sanctification is not primarily accomplished through relentless self examination or anxious effort to fix every flaw we discover within ourselves. It happens as we behold Christ, gazing at who He is, meditating on His character revealed in Scripture, and allowing that vision to gradually reshape our own. We become like what we consistently look at and admire.
:::

## 3. Two Ditches

Philippians 2:12 and 13 holds together a paradox that captures the true balance. Paul urges believers to work out their own salvation with fear and trembling, language that implies genuine effort, discipline, and seriousness. But he immediately grounds that effort in a deeper reality: for it is God who works in you both to will and to do for His good pleasure.

Notice that Paul does not say work *for* your salvation. He says work *out* what God has already worked *in*.

| Ditch | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Passivity** | Waiting for growth to happen automatically, as though sanctification requires nothing of the believer |
| **Self-reliance** | Treating growth as an achievement to white-knuckle through sheer discipline, functionally forgetting the grace that made any growth possible |

Philippians 2:12 and 13 refuses both extremes. Genuine effort, genuine cooperation, genuine discipline, all flowing from and dependent upon a work God Himself is already doing.

## 4. Not Alone

Sanctification, though deeply personal, was never designed to happen in isolation. The New Testament consistently places spiritual growth within committed relationships among believers, describing the church as a body whose individual members depend on one another for proper function.

Hebrews urges believers not to forsake gathering together, but to encourage one another, considering how to stir one another toward love and good works. Iron sharpens iron.

:::note Why isolation stalls growth
God has provided the encouragement, correction, and example of other believers as one of His primary means of shaping us, and neglecting that community often stalls growth that would otherwise flourish within it.
:::

Paul describes the goal concretely: this is the will of God, your sanctification (1 Thessalonians 4:3). Practical holiness is not an optional add-on for especially devoted believers. It is the expressed will of God for every person who has trusted in Christ.

## 5. Fruit, Not Product

Paul describes the visible outcome using the language of fruit rather than manufactured product, and the distinction matters.

A factory produces goods through mechanical effort applied directly to raw material. A tree produces fruit through a much quieter process, drawing on roots, sunlight, and time, the fruit appearing as the natural overflow of a healthy, living organism rather than the result of the fruit straining to produce itself.

:::warn The distinction that keeps assurance intact
Sanctification is the fruit of salvation, never its foundation. A believer does not become more saved through growing holiness, and a believer does not become less saved through seasons of failure or slow progress. The legal reality established at justification remains constant throughout the entire uneven journey.
:::

And this security, rather than undermining motivation for holiness, is actually what makes genuine holiness possible. Only a person who is not anxiously trying to earn or maintain their standing with God is free to pursue holiness for its true purpose, growing love for God and neighbour, rather than as a means of self-justification.

## 6. Glorification: The Destination

Paul provides the clearest outline of the entire trajectory in Romans 8:29 and 30, often called the golden chain because of how tightly its links are bound. Whom He foreknew, He also predestined... whom He predestined, these He also called, whom He called, these He also justified, and whom He justified, these He also **glorified**.

Notice something remarkable about the grammar. Paul describes glorification using the same past tense he uses for justification, even though believers have not yet experienced it.

:::quote Chapter Thirteen
This is not a grammatical accident. Paul is expressing such confidence in the certainty of this future reality that he speaks of it as already accomplished, as secure and settled in God's purposes as anything that has already happened.
:::

Unlike sanctification, glorification is not experienced gradually or unevenly. It is a decisive, instantaneous transformation at Christ's return, when believers receive resurrected bodies no longer subject to sin, sickness, aging, or death.

John admits an honest limitation alongside a glorious certainty. It has not yet been revealed what we shall be, but we know that when He is revealed, we shall be like Him, for we shall see Him as He is (1 John 3:2). John does not pretend to understand every detail. What he knows with absolute confidence is that seeing Christ as He truly is will itself be the catalyst for our final transformation.

## 7. Bodily, and Beyond Eden

It matters that biblical hope centres on bodily resurrection rather than a purely spiritual existence detached from physical reality. Some ancient philosophies viewed the body as a prison from which the soul longed to escape. That was never the biblical vision. God made bodies, declared them good, and the incarnation demonstrates how highly He regards physical existence, since the eternal Son retains a glorified human body even now.

Glorification is not an escape from physical existence but its full and final redemption.

Revelation pulls back the curtain on the destination: a new heaven and a new earth, the tabernacle of God with men, and God wiping away every tear, with no more death, nor sorrow, nor crying, nor pain.

:::quote Chapter Thirteen
What God is preparing for His people is not a return to the garden but an advance beyond it, a world secured forever against the very possibility of the tragedy described in chapter three of this book.
:::

Revelation's vision includes no serpent, no tree of the knowledge of good and evil offering the possibility of a second fall, no shadow of doubt about God's character left to be exploited by a deceiver.

This confidence rests not in speculation but in the character and track record of the God who promised it. Philippians 1:6 summarizes it: He who has begun a good work in you will complete it until the day of Jesus Christ.

## 8. Assurance: Held by Two Hands

Can a believer actually know, with genuine confidence, that all of this is true for them? Scripture answers with a resounding yes, and grounds that confidence not in fluctuating feelings but in the unchanging promises and character of God.

Jesus offers one of the most comforting statements in the Bible. My sheep hear My voice, and I know them, and they follow Me. And I give them eternal life, and they shall never perish, neither shall anyone snatch them out of My hand. My Father, who has given them to Me, is greater than all, and no one is able to snatch them out of My Father's hand.

:::quote Chapter Fourteen
Believers are held both in the hand of the Son and in the hand of the Father. This is not a promise of security based on the believer's own grip strength, their ability to hold onto God through their own effort and vigilance. It is a promise based on God's grip.
:::

## 9. Romans 8 Closes Every Door

Paul offers the most triumphant expression of assurance in Scripture at the close of Romans 8, erupting into rhetorical questions that leave no room for doubt.

- **If God is for us, who can be against us?** He who did not spare His own Son, how shall He not with Him also freely give us all things? Paul argues from the greater to the lesser. If God gave His Son while we were still sinners, He will not withhold anything necessary to complete what He began.
- **Who shall bring a charge against God's elect?** It is God who justifies.
- **Who is he who condemns?** It is Christ who died, and furthermore is also risen, who is even at the right hand of God, who also makes intercession for us.
- **Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?** Neither death nor life, nor angels nor principalities nor powers, nor things present nor things to come, nor height nor depth, nor **any other created thing**.

Paul does not merely address a few obvious threats and leave the rest to speculation. He systematically eliminates every conceivable category: external circumstances, spiritual powers, time itself, and any other created thing whatsoever.

Ephesians adds the Spirit's role. Having believed, you were sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise, who is the guarantee of our inheritance. In the ancient world a seal represented ownership, authenticity, and protection.

## 10. Two Grounds of Assurance

It helps to distinguish two related but different grounds on which assurance rests.

| | Objective ground | Subjective ground |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | The finished, unchanging work of Christ recorded in Scripture | The inward witness of the Spirit and the observable fruit of a changed life |
| **Stability** | True whether or not a believer feels it on any given day | Fluctuates with circumstances, seasons of dryness, and ordinary ups and downs |

Mature assurance rests its full weight on the objective ground while welcoming the subjective as confirmation. A believer who builds assurance primarily on feelings will rise and fall with them. A believer who builds on the finished work of Christ has something that does not move.

## Reflection

Read Romans 8:31 to 39 aloud, slowly. Each time Paul names a threat, pause and name the specific fear in your own life that corresponds to it. Then read his conclusion aloud one more time.

## Summary

Sanctification is the one doctrine in Part Three that unfolds over a lifetime rather than settling in a moment, and Romans 12 makes clear it follows grace rather than earning it. It works chiefly through the renewal of the mind and by beholding Christ rather than by anxious self-inspection, and Philippians 2 refuses both passivity and white-knuckled self-reliance. It happens in community rather than isolation, and it is fruit rather than manufactured product, which means growth never adds to and failure never subtracts from the legal standing established at justification. That process is heading somewhere. Glorification is instantaneous, bodily, and so certain that Paul writes of it in the past tense, and what God is preparing is an advance beyond Eden rather than a return to it. All of this makes assurance possible: held in two hands, with every category of threat systematically eliminated in Romans 8, and resting its full weight on a finished work rather than a fluctuating feeling.

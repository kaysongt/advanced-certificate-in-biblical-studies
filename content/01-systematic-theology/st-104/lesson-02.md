Every great story needs a turning point, the moment when everything changes and nothing can go back to the way it was. For the human race, that moment happened in a garden, around a single tree, in a conversation that lasted only a few verses but has shaped every generation since.

This lesson covers the fall and the doctrine it produced. Dr. Kay warns that until we understand sin correctly, grace will never feel like the overwhelming gift it actually is.

:::objectives By the end of this lesson you will be able to
1. Analyse the strategy embedded in the serpent's opening question.
2. Trace the immediate aftermath of the fall and explain what the fig leaves establish about human remedies.
3. Explain the representative headship argument of Romans 5 and its bearing on hope.
4. Identify the threads of mercy running through the curses and the expulsion.
5. Explain how Genesis 3:21 previews the shape of the gospel.
6. Define sin as a condition rather than a catalogue of behaviours, using the biblical vocabulary and Psalm 51.
7. Explain why the doctrine of sin levels every ground of comparison between people.
:::

## Key Terms

:::terms
The Fall | | Humanity's rebellion in Genesis 3 and the consequences that followed for the whole created order.
Original Sin | | Not the claim that infants are guilty of specific crimes, but that every human being inherits a nature bent away from God which will inevitably express itself in actual sins.
Representative Headship | | The structure by which one person acts on behalf of many. Adam's disobedience and Christ's obedience both operate this way.
The Last Adam | | Paul's title for Christ in 1 Corinthians 15, contrasting the first Adam who brought death with the One who opened the way to resurrection life.
Protoevangelium | Latin, first gospel | Genesis 3:15, the first promise of a coming Redeemer, spoken in the middle of judgment.
Total Depravity | | The doctrine that sin has affected every part of human nature, leaving no faculty untouched. It does not mean every person is as wicked as they could possibly be.
:::

## 1. The Strategy in the Question

Genesis 3 opens with a question. The serpent asks, has God indeed said you shall not eat of every tree of the garden?

Notice the strategy embedded in that question. God had given Adam and Eve access to every tree in the garden except one. The serpent reframes abundant generosity as restrictive stinginess, planting a seed of doubt about God's character before a single bite of fruit is ever taken.

:::quote Chapter Three
Temptation still works this way. It rarely begins with an outright command to sin. It begins with a subtle reinterpretation of who God is. Is He really good? Is He really holding back something wonderful from you?
:::

The woman's response reveals how quickly doubt distorts truth. She adds a restriction God never gave, claiming they were forbidden even to touch the tree, and this small exaggeration becomes the crack through which further deception pours in. The serpent then flatly contradicts God, promising that eating will open their eyes to become like God. It is a lie wrapped around a partial truth, because their eyes would indeed be opened, just not as the serpent implied. They would see, for the first time, their own guilt and shame.

## 2. Fig Leaves and Hiding

Genesis 3:6 records the decision with almost unbearable simplicity. No lightning struck. No voice thundered from heaven in that instant. But something fractured in the fabric of creation that would never be the same.

Their eyes were opened, and they realized they were naked, and they sewed fig leaves together to cover themselves. Before sin, nakedness meant nothing, because there was nothing to hide. After sin, covering became instinctive.

:::warn The first religious act, and its failure
This is the first religious act in human history, and it is a failure. Fig leaves cannot cover guilt. Human effort to fix what sin broke never quite works, a pattern that will repeat throughout the rest of Scripture until Christ arrives to accomplish what no human effort ever could.
:::

Then comes the sound every honest reader recognizes instinctively, God walking in the garden in the cool of the day, and the couple hiding among the trees. The God who once walked with humanity in unbroken fellowship now calls out, *where are you*, not because He does not know their location, but because He is inviting them into the conversation their guilt is causing them to avoid.

## 3. Representative Headship

Paul summarizes the consequences with theological precision. Through one man sin entered the world, and death through sin, and thus death spread to all men (Romans 5:12).

This is a staggering claim. Adam's disobedience was not a private failure with private consequences. It was a representative failure, and its effects rippled outward to touch every person who would ever be born.

This can feel unfair at first hearing. Why should the choice of one man in a garden thousands of years ago affect me? But Paul is not finished. In the same passage he sets Adam and Christ side by side as two representative heads of humanity. Just as one man's disobedience made many sinners, one Man's obedience makes many righteous.

:::quote Chapter Three
The very structure of representative headship that explains how sin spread to the human race is the same structure that explains how righteousness can be freely given to us in Christ.
:::

Paul makes the comparison directly in 1 Corinthians 15. Christ is called the last Adam, a life-giving Spirit, in contrast to the first Adam, who became a living being. The first Adam was formed from the dust and, through disobedience, subjected creation to death. The last Adam came from heaven and, through obedience, opened the way to resurrection life.

## 4. Mercy Woven Into Judgment

Even the curses carry a strange thread of mercy running through them.

- The ground is cursed so that it will resist Adam's labour, but **labour itself remains**, giving Adam purpose rather than idle despair.
- Childbirth becomes painful, yet the **promise of children**, and eventually the promised Redeemer, remains intact.
- The expulsion from the garden, which sounds at first like pure punishment, is explained by the text itself as a **protective act**. God did not want humanity to eat from the tree of life in its fallen condition and live forever trapped in sin without hope of redemption.

Judgment and mercy are woven together from the very first pages of the fall, a pattern that will characterize the rest of Scripture all the way to the cross.

## 5. The Gospel Previewed

One detail deserves attention because it quietly previews the entire gospel in a single verse. Genesis 3:21 tells us that God Himself made tunics of skin for Adam and Eve and clothed them, replacing their inadequate fig leaves with a covering He provided.

This required the death of an animal, the first blood shed in Scripture, and it establishes a pattern that runs through the rest of the biblical story.

:::quote Chapter Three
Human effort to cover sin and shame always falls short. Only a covering provided by God, purchased at a cost He bears rather than we bear, actually suffices.
:::

And before all of this, in the middle of pronouncing judgment, God had already spoken of rescue. The seed of the woman would crush the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15). Even in the darkest moment of the story, before Adam and Eve had taken a single step out of the garden, God was already promising a Redeemer.

## 6. Sin Is a Condition, Not a List

It has become common, even inside the church, to speak about sin as though it were simply a list of bad behaviours we should try to avoid. That is true as far as it goes, and it barely scratches the surface.

The Bible describes sin using several different words, and each adds a layer.

| Image | What it conveys |
|---|---|
| Missing a target | Falling short of a standard we were meant to reach |
| Rebellion | An active turning away from rightful authority |
| Debt | An obligation owed that we are utterly unable to pay |

:::quote Chapter Four
Sin is not merely doing bad things. Sin is a condition, a fractured relationship with God at the deepest level of who we are, and that fractured relationship produces the bad behaviors we usually think of first.
:::

David understood this better than most. In Psalm 51, written after his affair with Bathsheba and the murder of her husband, he does not simply confess a series of actions. He confesses a condition: behold, I was brought forth in iniquity, and in sin my mother conceived me.

David is not blaming his mother. He is acknowledging that his capacity to sin did not begin the day he saw Bathsheba on the rooftop. It was present from the very beginning of his existence.

## 7. The Universal Scope

This is what theologians mean by original sin. It is not the claim that babies are guilty of specific crimes. It is the claim that every human being inherits a nature bent away from God, a nature that will inevitably express itself in actual sins the moment it has opportunity.

Romans 3:23 states the scope with stark simplicity. All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God. Not most people. Not the obviously wicked. All. No exceptions carved out for the moral, the religious, or the well intentioned.

Jesus Himself located the true source of sin not in outward circumstance but in the human heart. It is not what enters a person from the outside that defiles them, but what proceeds from within. This would have unsettled religious listeners accustomed to measuring righteousness by external compliance.

:::warn Why external reform cannot solve it
A heart bent away from God will eventually express that bentness in behavior, no matter how carefully the behavior is managed or disguised in the meantime.
:::

## 8. Separation and Wages

Isaiah adds a dimension often overlooked, explaining why sin is such a serious problem in relational rather than merely legal terms. Your iniquities have separated you from your God, and your sins have hidden His face from you (Isaiah 59:1 to 2).

Sin is not simply breaking a rule. It is a barrier, a wall that grows between us and the very Source of life, light, and hope.

Romans 6:23 states the consequence in a single unforgettable sentence. The wages of sin is death. *Wages* describes something earned, something we worked for and are owed. Death here is not limited to physical death. It describes a comprehensive separation from God, both now and eternally.

## 9. Why This Levels Everything

One reason this doctrine matters practically is that it removes every possible ground for comparison between people.

It is tempting to mentally sort ourselves into categories of relatively good and relatively bad, quietly assuming our own sins fall into a more forgivable tier. Paul's argument in Romans 3 will not permit this. The Jewish reader who trusted in the law and the Gentile reader who never had access to it are placed on precisely the same footing.

:::quote Chapter Four
This is actually good news in disguise, because it means the gospel is not a message reserved for people who have sinned less. It is a message for everyone, regardless of the specific shape their sin has taken.
:::

John offers both warning and promise. If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves. Honesty about our condition is not optional for genuine faith. But John does not stop at the diagnosis. If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness (1 John 1:8 to 10).

:::note How to preach this
The bad news of sin is only ever preached properly when it makes room for the good news of grace. If you have felt the weight of this lesson, take heart. You are exactly the kind of person the gospel was written for. God's grace is not reserved for people who have cleaned themselves up first.
:::

## Reflection

Read Psalm 51 in full. Note every place David speaks of condition rather than action. Write one sentence naming something in your own life you have been treating as a behaviour problem that is in fact a heart problem.

## Summary

The fall began not with an act but with a question that recast God's generosity as stinginess. Doubt distorted the command, the decision followed, and the immediate instinct was to cover and to hide. The fig leaves stand as the first religious act and the first failure. Paul explains the consequences through representative headship, which feels unjust until you notice that the same structure is what allows Christ's obedience to be credited to others. Mercy runs through the curses, through the protective expulsion, and above all through the covering God provided at the cost of the first blood shed in Scripture, immediately after the first promise of a Redeemer. Sin itself is a condition rather than a list, described as missing a mark, as rebellion, and as debt, confessed by David as something present from his beginning. It reaches all people without exception, which levels every comparison and makes the gospel a message for everyone. Lesson 3 turns to the answer.

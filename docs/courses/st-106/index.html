Study God's eternal plan for creation, including the personal return of Christ, the resurrection of the dead, final judgment, and the new heavens and new earth. Students examine the biblical teaching on heaven, hell, and the eternal state, along with the major views on end-time events presented with clarity and balance. This course inspires believers to live each day with hope, urgency, and an eternal perspective, anchored in the certainty of Christ's ultimate victory and return.

History is not spinning out of control. It is moving, deliberately and surely, toward the day when Jesus Christ will return in glory, when every wrong will be made right, and when everyone who has trusted him will be welcomed home.

How This Course Is Built

The textbook moves in three parts, and this course follows them.

Part One surveys the unseen realm: the angels who serve God's purposes, the adversary who opposes them, and the warfare a believer actually fights. Part Two traces the consummation of all things, from the moment of death through Christ's return, the resurrection, and the final judgment. Part Three turns to the eternal destinies themselves, heaven and hell, and then to the new creation and the life this hope requires today.

Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course the student will be able to

  1. Describe what angels are, what they do, and the three errors the doctrine is meant to prevent.
  2. Explain why no true angel in Scripture accepts worship, and what that refusal teaches.
  3. Account for Satan's origin, titles, and methods without granting him equality with God.
  4. Explain why demonic belief is not saving faith, using James 2:19.
  5. State the difference between fighting for victory and fighting from it, and locate that difference in the structure of Ephesians.
  6. Explain each piece of the armour of God as something already possessed rather than manufactured.
  7. Explain the intermediate state and the evidence against soul sleep.
  8. Distinguish grief without hope from grief anchored in hope.
  9. Defend the visible, personal, bodily return of Christ, and explain parousia and apokalypsis.
  10. Explain why date-setting undermines the very watchfulness Jesus commands.
  11. Explain the firstfruits argument of 1 Corinthians 15 and what a spiritual body is and is not.
  12. Distinguish the judgment seat of Christ from the great white throne.
  13. Explain how works function at judgment if salvation is by faith alone.
  14. Correct the popular picture of heaven from Revelation 21 and 22.
  15. Present the doctrine of hell soberly, including the strongest objection to it and Scripture's answer.
  16. Explain why the new creation is renewal rather than replacement, and what that implies for present labour.
  17. Name the fruits the hope of eternity is meant to produce in a believer's present life.

Course Assignment

Write 900 to 1,200 words on the following.

Dr. Kay states plainly that this is not a book of speculation, and he declines to stake out a position on the tribulation and the millennium because faithful interpreters disagree. Using at least four passages from this course, identify what he treats as the unshakable centre that every faithful view shares. Then explain what a congregation loses when the disputed details are taught as though they were the centre, and what it loses when eschatology is avoided altogether.

You are graded on scriptural grounding, on whether you have correctly identified the centre rather than substituting a preferred system for it, and on the specificity of both failure cases.

Instructor Notes

This course carries more emotional weight than any other in the module. Students arrive with recent grief, with fears about a loved one who died without a clear profession, and with inherited end-times charts they may be more attached to than they realise. Plan for that.

Two disciplines hold the course together. The first is Dr. Kay's refusal to speculate where Scripture is silent, which comes up sharply in Lesson 3 when students want a position on the rapture and tribulation sequence. Hold the line he holds: name the views, refuse to adjudicate, and return to the centre. The second is the pastoral tone required in Lesson 5. He writes that any presentation of hell that seems eager or comfortable has misunderstood something essential, and that a believer who speaks of it with glee has misunderstood the God who revealed it. Model that tone rather than merely describing it.

Lesson 1 tends to draw the most questions of idle curiosity, particularly about guardian angels and the identity of the demonic. Use the textbook's own restraint as the answer: it warns explicitly against building more theology on a single verse than it can bear.

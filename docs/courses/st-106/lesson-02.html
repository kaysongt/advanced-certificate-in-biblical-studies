If the previous lesson left the impression that the enemy is finished and there is nothing more to think about, this one exists to correct it. Satan's defeat at the cross is total and certain, but it has not yet been fully applied to every corner of this present age. There is still opposition to face and still a call in Scripture to stand firm.

The second half of the lesson turns to the subject that brings people to Scripture with more urgency than any other. Every person reading this will die unless the Lord returns first, and every person reading this has watched someone they love walk through that door ahead of them.

Key Terms

Spiritual warfare
The believer's conflict with spiritual opposition, fought from a position already won rather than toward a victory still in doubt.
Armour of God
The six items of Ephesians 6, each a description of the gospel viewed from a different angle, worn consciously rather than manufactured.
Intermediate state
The period between an individual's death and the resurrection of the body at Christ's return.
Soul sleep
The view that the dead remain unconscious until the resurrection, which the textbook rejects on the evidence of several passages.
The last enemy
Paul's description of death in 1 Corinthians 15:26, refusing to treat it as natural, neutral, or a friend.
Grief with hope
Not the absence of grief but its transformation; Christians cry at funerals, but not as those who believe this is the ending of the story.

1. Fighting From, Not For

The difference between biblical spiritual warfare and every counterfeit version of it comes down to a single question. Are you fighting for victory, or from victory. Scripture is emphatic that the believer fights from a position already won.

The structure of Ephesians proves the point. Paul spends the first half of the letter describing everything believers already possess in Christ: chosen before the foundation of the world, adopted as sons, redeemed through His blood, sealed with the Holy Spirit, raised up and seated with Him in the heavenly places. Only after laying that foundation does he turn, in chapter six, to warfare. And even there his first instruction is not to attack but to stand.

Four times in a few short verses he repeats some form of the word stand. This is not the language of an army advancing to seize new territory. It is the language of an army holding ground that has already been won.
Chapter Three

2. What Kind of Conflict This Is

Our struggle, Paul writes, is not against flesh and blood but against the rulers, the authorities, the cosmic powers over this present darkness. This is one of the most important sentences in the Bible for understanding how a believer should interpret conflict, and it is also one of the most frequently abused.

Two ways to misread Ephesians 6:12

It does not mean that every difficult person in your life is a demon in disguise, and it would be a serious mistake to treat other human beings that way. Nor does it license blaming other people for spiritual battles they are not fighting. What it means is that behind the visible tensions of life there is an unseen dimension of opposition our natural categories often miss entirely, and recognising it never excuses our own choices.

When you face a temptation that seems designed specifically to exploit your particular weakness, or a discouragement that arrives at exactly the moment you were beginning to bear fruit, Scripture gives you permission to recognise that more may be happening than meets the eye.

3. The Armour, Piece by Piece

Each item corresponds to something believers already have through union with Christ rather than something they must manufacture.

PieceWhat it is
Belt of truthA life ordered by reality rather than illusion; settled honesty that leaves no room for deception
Breastplate of righteousnessNot a performance achieved by effort but Christ's righteousness credited through faith
Feet fitted with the gospel of peaceSure footing that comes from knowing you are at peace with God
Shield of faithSimple trust that extinguishes the flaming darts of doubt, fear, accusation, despair
Helmet of salvationProtection for the mind, where most battles are actually won or lost
Sword of the SpiritThe word of God, the one offensive weapon, and the one Jesus used in the wilderness

The shield image is drawn from ancient warfare, where soldiers soaked their shields to douse fire-tipped arrows before they could catch and spread. The sword is the weapon Jesus Himself used against Satan, answering each temptation not with clever argument but with Scripture spoken plainly.

Notice that every piece of this armor is essentially a description of the gospel itself, viewed from different angles. There is no secret formula here, no special technique reserved for spiritual experts.
Chapter Three

Spiritual warfare in its most basic biblical form is the discipline of remembering and standing on what is already true, refusing to let the enemy's lies displace the reality secured by the cross and the empty tomb.

4. The Order in James

James gives the simplest practical instruction anywhere in Scripture. Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you.

Notice the order. Submission comes first, and resistance follows from it, not the other way around. A believer who tries to resist the devil in his own strength, without first submitting fully to God, is fighting with no foundation underneath him. But the promise attached to obedient resistance is remarkable in its simplicity. He will flee, as the natural response of a defeated enemy confronted by someone standing in the authority Christ has given.

Peter supplies the balance: be sober minded and watchful, because your adversary prowls like a roaring lion. That is the warning. Then the remedy: resist him, firm in your faith, knowing that the same sufferings are being experienced by your brotherhood throughout the world.

There is no isolation in this fight

Peter's mention of the worldwide brotherhood is not a throwaway line. Knowing that others face the same opposition is itself part of how believers stand, rather than assuming their difficulty is uniquely severe or evidence that God has abandoned them.

John gives the shortest and most confident statement on the whole subject: He who is in you is greater than he who is in the world. And Paul closes every door the enemy might use, naming death, life, angels, rulers, things present, things to come, powers, height, depth, and anything else in all creation. That list is comprehensive on purpose.

5. Where Death Came From

Scripture is honest about this. Death was not part of God's original design. Genesis describes a creation pronounced very good, a garden without decay, a relationship unbroken by sin. Death entered through disobedience, as Paul explains: sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin.

This is why death, even when it comes peacefully at the end of a long life, is never treated in Scripture as simply natural or neutral. Paul calls it, bluntly, the last enemy to be destroyed.

An enemy, not a friend, not a release, not a natural transition to be celebrated on its own terms. Christianity does not romanticize death. It confronts it honestly as the intrusion it always was.
Chapter Four

And yet the same Scripture that names death an enemy declares it decisively wounded. Paul taunts it directly, asking where its sting has gone, before answering his own question. The sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law, but thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ. Death still happens. For the believer it has been stripped of its power to have the last word.

6. The Moment a Believer Dies

Scripture does not devote enormous space to the intermediate state, but what it does say is remarkably clear and consistently comforting.

Jesus, speaking to the thief crucified beside Him, a man with no time left to demonstrate a changed life through works or ministry, said simply: today you will be with Me in Paradise. Not someday. Not after a long wait in an undefined state. Today.

Paul writes to the Philippians of being torn between remaining to serve and departing to be with Christ, which he calls far better. He does not describe departure as a frightening unknown or an extended sleep but as an immediate improvement, better by far than even the fruitful ministry he was engaged in. To the Corinthians he expresses confidence that to be absent from the body is to be present with the Lord.

7. The Case Against Soul Sleep

Some hold that the dead remain unconscious until the resurrection. This reading struggles with the passages above and struggles more with two others.

Revelation shows John seeing under the heavenly altar the souls of those slain for the word of God, crying out with a loud voice, asking the Sovereign Lord how long until He judges and avenges their blood. They are given white robes and told to rest a little longer. These are not unconscious beings. They are aware, waiting, able to speak.

The Transfiguration adds a second window. Moses and Elijah appear, speaking with Jesus about His coming departure at Jerusalem. Moses had died centuries earlier, and both appear as recognisable, conscious, communicative beings.

What about those who die outside of Christ?

Scripture is more sobering here, and Topic 5 returns to it. But the account of the rich man and Lazarus presents the man in torment immediately after death, fully conscious, able to see, to feel, to remember, to plead, and to be refused. Death does not end conscious existence for anyone. It transitions a person, immediately and without an unconscious gap, into either the presence of Christ or separation from Him.

8. A Waystation, Not the Destination

One clarification prevents a common distortion. The Christian hope has never been merely about a disembodied soul floating in some ethereal paradise forever.

Being with Christ immediately upon death is wonderful and real, but Scripture presents it as a waystation rather than a final destination. Paul describes believers groaning inwardly, waiting eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies. The full hope includes a resurrected, glorified body reunited with the soul in a renewed creation. Topic 3 takes that up.

9. How Christians Grieve

Believers grieve, and Scripture never asks them not to. Jesus wept at the tomb of Lazarus knowing He was about to raise him moments later. Grief in the presence of death is not a failure of faith. It is a fitting response to an enemy that still, for now, intrudes into a world God intended for life.

What Paul asks is not the absence of grief but a particular quality of it. He tells the Thessalonians he does not want them uninformed about those who have fallen asleep, so that they may not grieve as others do who have no hope.

The contrast is not between grief and no grief. It is between grief without hope and grief anchored in hope. Christians cry at funerals. They simply do not cry the way people cry who believe this is the final ending of the story.
Chapter Four

Dr. Kay is careful not to overclaim here. Scripture does not answer every private question about what dying will feel like, or about loved ones who died without a clear profession of faith, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. What it gives is the repeated assurance that God is near in the valley of the shadow. David walked through it without fear precisely because the Shepherd's presence, not the absence of darkness, was what secured him.

Reflection

Read 1 Thessalonians 4:13 slowly. Write one sentence describing a loss you carry, and a second sentence describing what changes about that grief when it is anchored rather than removed.

Summary

Biblical warfare is fought from victory rather than for it, which is why Ephesians establishes what believers already possess before it ever mentions the enemy, and why Paul's repeated command is to stand. Each piece of the armour is the gospel seen from a different angle, already given rather than manufactured, with Scripture as the single offensive weapon. James fixes the order as submission first, then resistance; Peter adds sobriety without preoccupation and the reminder that the whole brotherhood faces the same opposition; John settles the anxiety in one sentence. Death, meanwhile, is treated honestly as an enemy that entered through sin rather than as a natural transition, and just as honestly as an enemy already stripped of its sting. The believer who dies is immediately with Christ, as Jesus promised the thief and Paul expected for himself, and the awake souls of Revelation 6 together with the Transfiguration tell against any notion of unconscious sleep. That presence is a waystation rather than the destination, since the full hope includes the body. And grief remains, transformed rather than forbidden. Topic 3 turns to the return and the resurrection that complete the hope.

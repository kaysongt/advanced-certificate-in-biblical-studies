The story the Bible tells does not end with souls escaping a ruined world into some distant, immaterial heaven, leaving the earth behind as a discarded shell. It ends with God renewing creation itself, making all things new rather than throwing them away and starting from scratch.

That distinction matters more than it first appears, because it shapes how believers understand their relationship to the physical world, to their own bodies, to their work, and to the destiny of everything God originally called very good. The lesson then closes the course by asking the question every previous lesson has been building toward. Now that we know where history is headed, how then should we live today.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. State what Isaiah anticipates about the new creation, including its extension to the animal kingdom.
  2. Explain why Peter's language of dissolution does not amount to annihilation.
  3. Explain Romans 8 on creation's groaning, and the significance of the childbirth image.
  4. Explain the symbolic weight of the sea being no more.
  5. Explain the difference between all things new and all new things, and why it matters.
  6. Explain what renewal implies for present labour, bodies, beauty, and craftsmanship.
  7. Name the seven fruits the hope of eternity is meant to produce, with a supporting passage for each.
  8. Explain Peter's question in 2 Peter 3:11 and why eschatology was never meant to be merely interesting.

Key Terms

New heavens and new earth
The renewed creation promised by Isaiah, Peter, and Revelation, in which righteousness dwells.
Renewal rather than replacement
The textbook's central claim about the new creation: God makes all things new rather than making all new things.
The sea was no more
Revelation 21:1. Throughout Scripture the sea often represents chaos and threat; its absence signals a world free of every remnant of disorder.
Creation's groaning
Romans 8, where creation is personified as subjected to futility and waiting, alongside believers, for its own liberation.
EschatologyGreek eschatos, last

1. What the Prophets Anticipated

Isaiah records God's promise: I create new heavens and a new earth, and the former things shall not be remembered or come into mind. He describes overflowing joy, with no more sound of weeping or cry of distress, and a place where those who build houses will inhabit them and those who plant vineyards will eat their fruit, an image directly contrasting the futility and disappointment that so often characterise labour in a fallen world.

He extends the renewal even into the animal kingdom, describing the wolf and the lamb grazing together and the lion eating straw like the ox, a vision of the entire created order restored to the peace it was designed to enjoy before sin introduced predation, decay, and death into a world never meant to know them.

2. Dissolution Is Not Annihilation

Peter picks up the same hope, describing the day of the Lord in cataclysmic terms: the heavens passing away with a roar, the heavenly bodies burned up and dissolved, the earth and its works exposed.

This language has sometimes led readers to conclude that the present creation will be annihilated and replaced with something entirely unrelated. But Peter's own conclusion points elsewhere. He does not end with annihilation. He ends with hope: according to His promise we are waiting for new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells.

Whatever the exact nature of the transformation Peter describes, the outcome is not the abolition of creation but its purification, a refining fire that burns away corruption while preserving and perfecting what God originally made.
Chapter Ten

3. Creation Groaning

Paul develops the theme with striking tenderness. The creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God, having been subjected to futility, not willingly, in hope that it will be set free from its bondage to corruption and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God.

This is a remarkable picture: creation itself personified as groaning under the weight of a curse it never chose, waiting alongside believers for the day of its own liberation.

Why the childbirth image is hopeful

Paul compares this groaning to the pains of childbirth, an image carrying its own built-in hope. Labour pains, however intense, are not a sign of things ending badly. They are the necessary prelude to new life arriving.

4. The Sea Was No More

Revelation's climactic vision brings it all together. John sees a new heaven and a new earth, for the first heaven and the first earth had passed away, and the sea was no more.

That final detail carries symbolic weight beyond its literal meaning, since throughout Scripture the sea often represents chaos, danger, and the unknown forces threatening God's ordered creation. Its absence signals a world finally and completely free from every remnant of disorder, danger, and threat.

Then the voice from the throne announces that the dwelling place of God is with man, followed immediately by a list of everything that will finally be undone: death, mourning, crying, pain. These are not decorative details. They are the precise wounds sin inflicted on the original very good creation, and each is described as undone, completely and permanently.

The story that began with a wound in Eden ends with that wound fully and finally healed.
Chapter Ten

5. All Things New, Not All New Things

The vision culminates in a statement worth pausing over precisely because of what it does not say. God does not say He is making all new things, discarding the old creation entirely and replacing it with something unrelated. He says He is making all things new, renewing what already exists rather than abandoning it.

This distinction runs consistently through Scripture's treatment of redemption. Salvation from beginning to end is a story of restoration rather than replacement, whether we are speaking of individual believers, made new creations in Christ while remaining genuinely themselves, or of the cosmos as a whole.

Replacement view What Scripture describes
The earth Discarded and rebuilt from nothing Purified and renewed
The body Exchanged for a different one Transformed, continuous with the present body
Present labour Ultimately irrelevant Not ultimately meaningless; it matters to God
The narrative arc Escape from creation Redemption of creation

The same logic that governs the resurrection of the body governs the renewal of the earth. God is not in the business of discarding what He has made. He is in the business of redeeming it, restoring it, and ultimately perfecting it beyond even its original unfallen state, since the new creation, unlike Eden, will never again be vulnerable to another fall.

6. What This Means for Monday

If the ultimate destiny of creation is renewal rather than destruction, then work done now, faithful stewardship of the physical world, honest labour, the creation of beauty, the cultivation of relationships and communities, is not merely killing time until an escape into an immaterial eternity.

Do not overclaim here

This does not mean believers should expect their specific unfinished projects to be picked up exactly where they left off in eternity, since Scripture does not promise anything that detailed. It does mean that the present physical world, and the labour believers invest in caring for it and each other within it, is not destined for the scrap heap.

Genesis begins with God placing His image bearers in a garden, walking among them in the cool of the day, and Revelation ends with God's dwelling place established permanently among a renewed humanity in a renewed creation. The garden and the city are the same story told at its beginning and at its end, except that the ending is richer, fuller, and more secure, because it can never again be lost.

The pursuit of beauty, the careful tending of a garden, the building of something well made and lasting, the composing of music or the writing of a poem, none of these things are merely distractions from spiritual life. They are small, imperfect echoes of the very renewal God himself intends to bring to completion.
Chapter Ten

7. The Question Peter Asks

Every doctrine explored in this course, from the ministry of angels to the renewal of heaven and earth, was never intended to remain an abstract subject for theological curiosity. Biblical truth about the future is always given for the sake of transforming how believers live in the present.

Peter asks it directly, after describing the dissolution of the present heavens and earth and the promise of new ones: since all these things are thus to be dissolved, what sort of people ought you to be in lives of holiness and godliness.

Eschatology, the study of last things, was never meant to be merely intellectually interesting. It was meant to be morally and spiritually productive.
Chapter Eleven

8. Seven Fruits of This Hope

Fruit Passage The connection
Holiness 1 John 3:2 to 3 Everyone who hopes in Him purifies himself, practising in advance for the perfection that is coming
Perseverance 1 Corinthians 15:58 Because your labour will be vindicated rather than forgotten, you can keep labouring when results are hard to see
Courageous witness 2 Corinthians 5:11, 14 Knowing the fear of the Lord, Paul persuades; the love of Christ controls him
Faithful stewardship Matthew 25:14 to 30 The servant who buried his talent out of fear is condemned; everything entrusted is to be invested
Contentment Philippians 4:11 to 13 Paul writes from prison, grounding contentment in Christ rather than circumstances
Freedom from possessions Matthew 6:19 to 21; 1 Timothy 6:17 to 19 Holding things loosely, using them generously, storing up a good foundation for the future
Joy 1 Peter 1:6 to 9 Rejoicing with joy inexpressible even while grieved by various trials

Two of these deserve a further word.

Perseverance is not gritted-teeth optimism. It is confidence grounded in a settled knowledge of how the story ends. Paul's therefore in 1 Corinthians 15:58 ties the exhortation directly to everything he has just said about the resurrection.

And the freedom regarding possessions is not a call to poverty or to disengagement from responsible work, since Scripture elsewhere commands diligent labour and generous provision for one's family. It is a call to a particular posture toward material things, refusing to let them become the source of identity or security they were never designed to be.

What contentment actually rests on

A believer who has grasped the reality of an inheritance Peter describes as imperishable, undefiled, and unfading finds it far easier to hold present disappointments and even genuine sufferings with a stability that would otherwise be impossible. This world's setbacks, however painful, are not the final chapter.

9. Where the Course Ends

The Christian faith is a faith of hope, and that hope is not vague optimism about the future but confident certainty grounded in what God has already done in Christ and promised to complete. He has defeated the powers of darkness at the cross. He has conquered death through the resurrection. He is coming again, visibly and gloriously, to raise the dead, to judge with perfect justice, and to make all things new.

Live today, then, not as someone anxious about an uncertain future, but as someone who already knows how the story ends, and who has been invited, by grace alone, to be part of its glorious final chapter. Be steadfast. Be faithful. Keep your eyes fixed on Christ.
Chapter Eleven

History is not drifting. Every promise explored in this course will be fulfilled. Every enemy that has ever opposed God's people will be finally defeated. Every tear that has ever fallen from grief, loss, injustice, or pain will be personally wiped away by the hand of the God who allowed none of it to be wasted and who is even now preparing a home where none of it will ever be repeated.

Until that day the church does not wait passively or anxiously. She lives with hope, standing firm in the victory Christ has already secured. She proclaims the gospel with confidence, knowing what is genuinely at stake. And she watches, eagerly, for the appearing of our great God and Saviour, echoing the ancient prayer that has risen from believers in every generation since the words were first written down.

Even so, come, Lord Jesus.

Summary

Scripture's ending is not escape from a ruined world but the renewal of creation itself. Isaiah anticipates a new heavens and new earth extending even to the animal kingdom; Peter's cataclysmic language resolves not in annihilation but in a purifying fire and a promise; Paul hears creation groaning like a woman in labour, which is a hopeful image rather than a despairing one; and John sees the sea, that standing symbol of chaos, gone. The decisive phrase is all things new rather than all new things, and the same logic that governs the resurrection of the body governs the renewal of the earth, which means present labour, bodies, beauty, and craftsmanship are echoes of a renewal God intends to complete rather than distractions from it. Peter then turns the whole doctrine into a question about character, and the course closes with the seven fruits this hope is meant to produce: holiness, perseverance, courageous witness, faithful stewardship, contentment, freedom from possessions, and joy. History is not drifting. It is moving toward the day when every promise is kept and every tear is wiped away by hand, and the church waits for it not passively but with hope, proclamation, and watchfulness. Even so, come, Lord Jesus.

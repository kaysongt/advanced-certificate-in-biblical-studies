What Is Theology?

Why doctrine belongs to every believer, not only to scholars

Strip away the academic packaging and look at the word itself. Theology comes from two Greek words, theos, meaning God, and logos, meaning word or study. At its simplest, theology is the study of God. If that is true, then every believer who has ever prayed, ever opened a Bible, ever asked why God allowed something to happen, has already been doing theology. The only question is whether we are doing it well.

That is where this course begins, and it is a deliberate starting point. Students often arrive at a systematic theology module assuming they are about to learn a specialist vocabulary. What they are actually about to do is take responsibility for something they have been doing informally their whole Christian life.

By the end of this topic you will be able to

  1. Define theology from its Greek roots and explain what systematic theology adds to that definition.
  2. Explain why every believer is already engaged in theology, and what is at stake in doing it carelessly.
  3. State the four reasons the textbook gives for why theology matters practically, and give a scriptural basis for each.
  4. Describe the relationship between Scripture and church history without treating tradition as a second authority.
  5. Identify the test by which the health of a person's theology can be measured.

Key Scriptures

And this is eternal life, that they may know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent.
John 17:3

Notice what Jesus does here. Eternal life is not defined as a destination we go to when we die. It is defined as a relationship of knowing. And you cannot know someone you have refused to study. This single verse is the reason theology cannot be optional.

  • John 17:3 — eternal life defined as knowing God
  • 1 Corinthians 8:1 to 3 — knowledge that puffs up against love that builds up
  • Acts 17:22 to 31 — Paul doing theology in the marketplace at Athens
  • 2 Timothy 3:16 to 17 — Scripture given for teaching, reproof, correction, training
  • 2 Timothy 4:3 to 4 — a time coming when people will not endure sound doctrine
  • 1 Peter 3:15 — always ready to give a defense, with gentleness and respect
  • Romans 11:33 to 36 — doctrine ending in doxology

Key Terms

TheologyGreek theos + logos
The study of God. In its broadest sense, any thinking a person does about God, whether careful or careless.
Systematic Theology
The discipline that organizes the teaching of Scripture topically rather than book by book, gathering everything the Bible says on a subject from Genesis to Revelation and synthesizing it into one coherent picture.
DoctrineLatin doctrina, teaching
The settled content of what the church believes and teaches, drawn from Scripture.
DoxologyGreek doxa + logos, glory-word
An outburst of praise. In Scripture, the characteristic ending of sound theological reasoning, as in Romans 11:33 to 36.
OrthodoxyGreek orthos + doxa, right belief
Teaching that conforms to what Scripture reveals and the church has consistently confessed.

1. Theology Is Not Optional

The most important sentence in this chapter is a claim about ownership.

Theology is not an optional pursuit reserved for scholars and pastors. It is the inheritance of every believer.
Chapter One

Dr. Kay presses the point with an observation worth sitting with. Some believers say that doctrine does not matter, that only a relationship with Jesus matters. But that person has not actually escaped theology. They have simply adopted an unexamined one, and unexamined theology tends to be shallow theology. There is no neutral ground here. Every believer holds convictions about God's character, God's power, and God's disposition toward them. The only variable is whether those convictions have been tested against Scripture.

This has a pastoral edge. Believers who have never been taught to think clearly about God do not thereby have less theology. They have a theology assembled from song lyrics, half-remembered sermons, cultural assumptions, and their own experience of their earthly father. That theology will hold or it will collapse, and it will usually collapse at the worst possible moment.

2. Answering the Suspicion of Doctrine

Many students carry a genuine hesitation into this subject, and it deserves an honest hearing rather than a dismissal. Some Christians have watched people argue about doctrine with more heat than love, treating disagreements over secondary matters as though they were matters of eternal life and death. Others have sat through teaching that felt more like a lecture on ancient philosophy than an encounter with the living God.

The textbook does not defend that. It makes a distinction instead.

The answer to bad theology has never been no theology. The answer is better theology, theology that is rigorous in its faithfulness to Scripture and warm in its posture toward the church.
Chapter One

Rigor and warmth are presented here as partners rather than opposites. A course that produces students who are precise and cold has failed as surely as one that produces students who are affectionate and imprecise.

Paul models the combination in Athens. Standing in a city saturated with philosophy and idolatry, he did not dismiss the Athenians' religious impulse. He engaged it, noting their altar to an unknown god and using it as a bridge to declare the God who made the world and everything in it, who does not live in temples built by human hands, and who commands all people everywhere to repent (Acts 17:22 to 31). Paul did theology in the marketplace, not merely in the synagogue. He treated clear thinking about God as one of evangelism's most powerful tools.

3. Four Reasons Theology Matters

These four run through the entire textbook, and a student should be able to state them from memory with a supporting text for each.

Theology strengthens faith. Faith that has never been tested by careful thought is fragile, the kind that crumbles at the first hard question a skeptic raises or the first tragedy that shakes a person's assumptions about God. A believer who has wrestled honestly with what Scripture teaches about God's sovereignty, goodness, and presence in suffering is far better equipped to stand when life gets difficult.

Theology shapes Christian living. What you believe about God inevitably shapes how you live before Him. A shrunken view of God's holiness produces careless living. A distorted view of His love produces either presumption or despair. Right belief and right living are never as separate as we sometimes imagine.

Theology protects against error. Paul warned Timothy that a time would come when people would not endure sound doctrine but would gather teachers to suit their own desires, turning away from truth toward myths (2 Timothy 4:3 to 4). The church has never been immune to false teaching and never will be. Believers who know what Scripture actually teaches are far less likely to be swept along by every new spiritual fad that promises freedom but delivers bondage.

Theology leads to worship. This is the test of whether our theology is healthy. Paul's great doctrinal survey in Romans, after eleven chapters of dense argument about sin, salvation, and the sovereignty of God, does not end with a diagram or a summary chart. It ends with an outburst of praise (Romans 11:33 to 36).

The diagnostic

If our study of God leaves us proud rather than humbled, argumentative rather than adoring, we have missed the point entirely, no matter how accurate our conclusions. Accuracy is necessary. It is not sufficient.

4. What Systematic Theology Actually Does

Systematic theology organizes the teaching of Scripture topically rather than simply moving verse by verse through a single book. Instead of studying only what Genesis says about God, or only what the Psalms say, it gathers everything Scripture teaches on a given subject, from Genesis to Revelation, and synthesizes it into a coherent, unified picture.

This method has two specific strengths worth naming.

  1. It guards against building an entire doctrine on a single, possibly misunderstood passage, because it forces us to weigh everything Scripture says on a subject before drawing conclusions.
  2. It helps believers see connections across the whole Bible that would remain hidden if we only ever studied one book or passage at a time.

The discipline is not a replacement for reading books of the Bible on their own terms. It is a complement to it.

5. Scripture, History, and the Humility of Receiving

When we study what the church has believed across the centuries, we are not adding a second source of authority alongside Scripture. Scripture alone remains the final and supreme rule for faith and practice. But history still matters, because the Holy Spirit has been at work in the church since Pentecost, and generations of believers before us have already wrestled with the same questions we wrestle with today.

When the church, across many centuries and many cultures, has consistently affirmed a particular understanding of a doctrine like the Trinity or the deity of Christ, that consensus deserves respectful attention, even as we continue to test every claim against Scripture itself.

Theology is not something each generation must invent from scratch. It is something each generation receives, tests, and passes on.
Chapter One

Notice all three verbs. Receiving without testing produces traditionalism. Testing without receiving produces arrogance. Both fail the generation that comes next, because neither leaves anything worth passing on.

6. Theology Is Not Primarily Defensive

There is a common misconception that theology is something Christians reach for only when they need to argue against a heretic or answer a skeptic. There is a place for that. Peter tells believers to always be ready to give a defense to anyone who asks a reason for the hope that is in them, doing so with gentleness and respect (1 Peter 3:15).

But if defense is the only reason we ever study God, we have reduced theology to something merely reactive. The overwhelming majority of Scripture was not written as a response to controversy. It was written to build up, comfort, instruct, and inspire worship among people who already believed. Healthy theology functions the same way, feeding the ordinary rhythms of faith long before it is ever called on to fight.

7. Doctrine and the Life of the Heart

Some people describe doctrine as though it were the opposite of feeling, as though the moment you start thinking carefully about God you must set aside your heart. Scripture shows the opposite pattern. David's psalms are saturated with careful theological reflection on God's character, and they are also some of the most emotionally raw prayers ever recorded, moving from anguish to confidence to praise sometimes within a single psalm. Job wrestles with enormous questions about suffering and divine justice through an entire book, and that wrestling is anything but clinical.

Right theology does not suppress emotion. It gives emotion a true and steady foundation to stand on, so that our feelings are shaped by reality rather than left to drift according to circumstance.
Chapter One

8. The Posture the Whole Course Assumes

Two sentences from the end of the chapter set the posture for everything that follows.

The first is about what theology is for. Paul told the Corinthians that if anyone thinks he knows anything, he does not yet know as he ought to know, but if anyone loves God, this one is known by Him (1 Corinthians 8:1 to 3). There is a kind of knowledge that puffs a person up with pride, and there is a kind that humbles a person into love. This course is aiming at the second kind.

The second is about direction of travel.

Theology, remember, is not primarily our search for God. It is our response to the God who has already come looking for us.
Chapter One

That sentence is the bridge into Topic 2. If theology is a response to God's initiative, the next question is unavoidable: how has He made Himself known?

Reflection

Write one paragraph, no more than 150 words, defining theology in your own words to a new believer who has told you they are not interested in doctrine. Do not use the words theos, logos, or systematic. Keep the paragraph — you will build on it in later topics.

Summary

Theology is the study of God, and by that definition every believer is already a theologian. The question is whether the work is being done carefully. Doctrine is the inheritance of every believer, not the property of specialists, and the remedy for theology done badly is theology done better rather than theology abandoned. It matters because it strengthens faith, shapes living, guards against error, and, above all, produces worship. That last item is the test. Systematic theology serves this work by gathering the whole of Scripture's teaching on a subject into one picture, and the church's historic consensus assists it without ever displacing Scripture's final authority. Above all, theology is a response rather than a search. God spoke first. Topic 2 asks how.

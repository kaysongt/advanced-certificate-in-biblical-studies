Part One established how God has spoken. Part Two asks who God is, and this lesson takes up His essential nature, meaning what kind of Being we are actually talking about when we say the word God.

Dr. Kay opens the chapter with a description of a moment rather than a definition.

There is a particular kind of silence that settles over a person the first time they genuinely grasp something true about the nature of God, not merely as a doctrine memorized for a test but as a reality that reorders everything else in their thinking.
Chapter Six

Keep that in view. The six truths in this lesson are not a checklist to be recited. Each is meant to reorder something.

By the end of this topic you will be able to

  1. Define aseity and explain what God's answer at the burning bush establishes about His being.
  2. Explain God's eternality and how it differs from merely existing for a very long time.
  3. Ground God's sovereignty in His self-existence, and use Isaiah 40 to recalibrate a discouraged believer's perspective.
  4. Explain why Scripture emphasizes holiness above every other attribute, and describe the pattern of Isaiah's response in Isaiah 6.
  5. Explain God's uniqueness and self-sufficiency, and state what follows for our understanding of why He created and redeemed us.
  6. Hold together God's transcendence and His personal nearness without diminishing either.

Key Scriptures

And God said to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM." And He said, "Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, 'I AM has sent me to you.'"
Exodus 3:14

God did not answer with a description borrowed from creation, the way a person might explain their identity in terms of their job or their family. He answered with pure, uncaused, self-existent being. Everything else exists because God spoke it into existence. God exists because God is.

Key Terms

AseityLatin a se, from himself
God's self-existence. His existence is not derived from, dependent on, or explained by anything outside of Himself.
Eternality
That God has no beginning and no end, and exists outside of and is not bound by the constraints of time as we experience it.
Sovereignty
God's ultimate authority and rule over everything that exists, flowing from the fact that He alone is uncaused and uncreated.
HolinessHebrew qadosh, set apart
God's absolute moral perfection and His complete otherness from creation, His fundamental separateness from everything sinful, common, or ordinary.
Self-sufficiency
That God needs nothing outside of Himself to be completely happy, complete, and satisfied.
Transcendence
That God is above and beyond creation, not contained by it.
Immanence
That God is genuinely present and near to His creation, and personally knowable.

1. Self-Existence

We have to begin with self-existence, sometimes called God's aseity. Every other thing that exists, from galaxies to atoms to human beings, owes its existence to something beyond itself. God alone simply is.

When Moses stood before the burning bush and asked God's name, the answer was startling in its simplicity and depth. God said, I AM WHO I AM, and instructed Moses to tell the people that I AM has sent him (Exodus 3:14).

This is worth pausing over, because it is the hinge on which the rest of the chapter turns. Everything else in the lesson follows from it. God is sovereign because He is uncaused. God is self-sufficient because His being is not derived. God is eternal because nothing brought Him into existence and nothing can remove Him from it.

2. Eternality

God's eternality is closely tied to His self-existence. He has no beginning and no end, and He is not bound by time as we experience it.

Moses, in one of the oldest psalms in Scripture, prayed that before the mountains were brought forth, or ever God had formed the earth and the world, even from everlasting to everlasting, God is God (Psalm 90:2).

Sit with that phrase, from everlasting to everlasting. There was never a point at which God came into being, and there will never be a point at which He ceases. He does not grow older. He does not look back on a past He can barely remember or forward toward a future He cannot yet see. He simply and eternally is, present to every moment of history at once.

Why this is pastoral rather than abstract

A God bound by time would experience your future as unknown and your past as receding. A God who is not bound by time holds the whole of your life, including the part you are dreading, in a single present view.

3. Sovereignty

Because God alone is uncaused and uncreated, He alone possesses ultimate authority and rule over everything that exists.

Isaiah addressed a discouraged people who felt forgotten by God with a series of pointed questions. Have you not known? Have you not heard? Has it not been told you from the beginning? It is He who sits above the circle of the earth, and its inhabitants are like grasshoppers (Isaiah 40:21 to 22).

That language is not meant to diminish human beings out of cruelty. It is meant to recalibrate perspective. When our problems feel enormous and God feels distant, Isaiah reminds us that the God we serve measures the oceans in the hollow of His hand, calls each star by name, and has understanding that is unsearchable (Isaiah 40:12, 26, 28).

Nothing that happens in this universe, however chaotic or frightening it may appear in the moment, happens outside His sovereign oversight.

4. Holiness

Holiness is perhaps the attribute Scripture emphasizes more than any other when describing God's essential nature.

When Isaiah was given a vision into the heavenly throne room, he did not hear the seraphim declaring God's love three times, or His power three times, though both are gloriously true. He heard them declaring holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts, the whole earth is full of His glory (Isaiah 6:3).

Holiness refers to God's absolute moral perfection and His complete otherness from creation. Isaiah's response is instructive. He did not respond with casual comfort but with terrified conviction, crying out, woe is me, for I am undone, because I am a man of unclean lips (Isaiah 6:5).

A genuine encounter with God's holiness always exposes our own uncleanness. Yet the vision does not end in condemnation. A seraph touches Isaiah's lips with a coal from the altar and declares his iniquity taken away (Isaiah 6:6 to 7).

Holiness that only condemns is not yet the full picture of the God revealed in Scripture. His holiness moves toward making sinners holy too.

5. Uniqueness

The great confession at the heart of Israel's faith, recited daily by faithful Jews for thousands of years and quoted by Jesus Himself as part of the greatest commandment, begins with these words: Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one (Deuteronomy 6:4).

In a world saturated with competing gods and idols, Israel was called to worship the one true God exclusively, not because He was the strongest among many rivals, but because He alone truly exists as God.

Paul reaffirms this in Athens, standing among altars built to a whole pantheon, declaring that the God who made the world and everything in it does not live in temples made with human hands, and is not served by human hands as though He needed anything, since He Himself gives to all life, breath, and all things (Acts 17:24 to 25).

Idolatry is rarely obvious

Every idol humanity has ever constructed, whether carved from stone or built from career achievement, relationship, or comfort, is ultimately an attempt to worship something manageable in place of the God who cannot be managed, contained, or controlled.

6. Self-Sufficiency and Why It Matters

Self-sufficiency means God needs nothing outside of Himself to be completely happy, complete, and satisfied. This sounds like a technical point. It carries enormous implications.

God did not create the world because He was lonely or incomplete without it. Within the eternal fellowship of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, God has always possessed perfect community, perfect love, and perfect joy, entirely within Himself.

We were never necessary additions to an incomplete God. We are the undeserved recipients of a God whose love simply chose to include us.
Chapter Six

This is one of the most freeing truths in the chapter. If God created and redeemed out of need, our value would rest on our usefulness to Him. Because He created and redeemed out of overflowing generosity, our value rests on His choice, which cannot be revoked by our failure to be useful.

7. The Transcendent God Who Comes Near

It is easy to read about self-existence, eternality, sovereignty, holiness, and uniqueness and walk away feeling that God is impossibly distant, too vast to be approached by someone like you.

Jesus said that eternal life is this, to know the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom He has sent (John 17:3). The God who spoke galaxies into existence and who measures the oceans in His palm has made Himself knowable, not through our striving upward but through His reaching downward.

Consider Elijah. One of the most powerful prophets in the Old Testament, fresh from a stunning public victory over the prophets of Baal, collapses into despair, flees into the wilderness, and becomes convinced he is the only faithful believer left. God's response was not a thunderous rebuke. After a great wind, an earthquake, and a fire, none of which contained the Lord's voice, Elijah finally heard a still, small voice, and it was there that God met him, questioned him gently, and recommissioned him (1 Kings 19:11 to 18).

The transcendent God who commands wind and fire chose, in that moment, to speak in a whisper to one exhausted, discouraged servant.

8. This Doctrine Under Pressure

The nature of God is tested most severely in suffering, and the chapter does not avoid it.

If God were merely powerful but not good, we would have reason to fear Him without trusting Him. If He were merely loving but not sovereign, we would have reason to sympathize with Him without being able to rely on Him to actually help. Scripture presents a God who is both perfectly sovereign and perfectly good, which means nothing touches our lives, including our deepest pain, without passing first through His wise and loving hands.

This does not mean we will always understand why particular suffering has been permitted. Job never received a direct explanation. Yet after encountering God's overwhelming greatness firsthand, he responded not with resentment but with humbled trust, saying he had heard of God by the hearing of the ear, but now his eye had seen Him (Job 42:5).

Sometimes the deepest comfort available to us in suffering is not an explanation but an encounter, a renewed sense of exactly who it is we are trusting.
Chapter Six

9. Where This Is Meant to Land

Everything in this lesson should produce worship rather than merely information. Theology that stops at accurate description without moving into adoration has fallen short of its purpose.

When Paul finished a long section describing God's ways as inscrutable and His judgments unsearchable, he did not follow it with a summary outline. He followed it with doxology, declaring that from Him and through Him and to Him are all things, to Him be glory forever (Romans 11:33, 36).

Read the next lesson not as a student memorizing facts for an exam, but as a worshiper standing, like Isaiah, before the throne.

Summary

God alone is self-existent, answering Moses not with a description but with pure being. From that root flows His eternality, His sovereignty over everything that exists, and His self-sufficiency, which means He created and redeemed out of generosity rather than need. Holiness is the attribute Scripture presses hardest, and a genuine encounter with it exposes our uncleanness and then moves toward cleansing. He is unique, the only true God, so every idol is an attempt to worship something more manageable. And this infinitely exalted God is personally knowable, meeting Elijah in a whisper and offering Job an encounter rather than an explanation. Held together, these truths are not information to be filed. They are meant to produce the doxology Paul reached for when argument ran out. Topic 5 turns from God's nature to His attributes.

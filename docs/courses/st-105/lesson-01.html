God's Called-Out People, and the Body of Christ

Who the Church is, and how she functions

Before we can talk about how the Church should worship, lead, or serve, we need to settle a more basic question. What is she?

The Greek word behind our English word church is ekklesia, and it refers to a gathering of people who have been called out from among the general population for a particular purpose. In the ancient Greek world the term described a legislative assembly, citizens summoned out of their homes and daily routines to deliberate on matters of public importance. When the New Testament writers borrowed this word, they were making a bold claim.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Define ekklesia and explain what the word claims about the Church's identity.
  2. Explain the significance of Christ's possessive language in Matthew 16:18.
  3. Distinguish the Church from the substitutes commonly mistaken for it.
  4. Explain the royal priesthood of all believers and what it abolishes.
  5. Explain the Body metaphor and both temptations Paul confronts with it.
  6. Explain why unity is preserved through diversity rather than uniformity.
  7. Explain the purpose of gifted leaders in Ephesians 4 and the phrase speaking the truth in love.

Key Scriptures

And I also say to you that you are Peter, and on this rock I will build My church, and the gates of Hades shall not prevail against it.
Matthew 16:18

Notice the possessive language. Jesus does not say He will build a church or support a church. He says My Church. Ownership belongs to Him. Authority belongs to Him. The blueprint belongs to Him.

  • Matthew 16:18 — I will build My church
  • Acts 2:41 to 47 — the earliest picture of the called-out community
  • Ephesians 2:19 to 22 — fellow citizens, built into a holy temple
  • 1 Peter 2:9 to 10 — a chosen generation, a royal priesthood
  • Romans 12:3 to 8 — sober self-assessment and the exercise of gifts
  • 1 Corinthians 12:12 to 27 — one body, many members
  • Ephesians 4:1 to 16 — gifted leaders equipping the saints
  • Colossians 1:18 — Christ is the head of the body

Key Terms

EkklesiaGreek, called out ones
A gathering summoned out from the general population for a particular purpose. In the ancient world, a legislative assembly.
Royal Priesthood
Peter's description of all believers, indicating direct access to God and priestly responsibility without a mediating class.
Living Stones
Peter's image, developed by Paul in Ephesians 2, of believers being fitted into a temple God Himself inhabits by His Spirit.
Body of Christ
Paul's primary metaphor for how the Church functions, emphasizing interdependence, diversity of function, and Christ as Head.
Interdependence
The mutual reliance of members such that if one part suffers, every part suffers with it.
Speaking the Truth in Love
Paul's phrase in Ephesians 4:15 for the balance by which the Body grows to maturity.

1. Called Out, Not Merely Improved

Peter tells scattered believers that they are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, and His own special people, and then explains why. It is so that they may proclaim the praises of Him who called them out of darkness into His marvellous light.

Notice the direction of the calling. God does not simply improve people where they stand. He calls them out. He relocates their allegiance from the kingdom of darkness to the Kingdom of His Son.
Chapter One

This calling did not originate with human beings deciding to organize themselves religiously. It originated with Jesus Himself. After Peter's confession, Jesus promised to build His Church, and that the gates of Hades would not prevail against it.

This means the Church is not a human institution that God happens to bless when it behaves well. The Church is Christ's own project, built according to His design, funded by His sacrifice, and populated by His choosing. Every attempt throughout history to reduce the Church to a social club, a political lobby, or a cultural relic has failed to reckon with this fact.

2. What the First Church Looked Like

Acts gives us the clearest picture. After Peter's sermon at Pentecost, three thousand were added in a single day. What happened next is instructive: they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching, to fellowship, to the breaking of bread, and to prayer. They shared everything. They met daily, in the temple courts and from house to house, eating with glad and sincere hearts.

What strikes me most about this description is how ordinary and how extraordinary it is at the same time. These were not people performing religious theater for an audience. Being called out did not mean being called away from ordinary life. It meant bringing Christ into every ordinary corner of it.
Chapter One

Paul develops the image further for the Ephesians. They are no longer strangers and foreigners but fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God, built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus Himself as the chief cornerstone.

A building is not finished the moment the foundation is poured. It grows, course by course, over time. You and I are living stones being fitted into a structure that spans centuries and continents.

There is comfort here for anyone who has ever felt like an outsider. Paul addresses Gentiles with no prior claim to the covenants of Israel and tells them they are now fellow citizens. There are no second-class citizens in God's household.

3. What the Church Is Not

It is worth distinguishing the Church from the substitutes people often mistake for it.

Not the Church Why
A building Believers often gather in one, but the Church exists without it
A denomination It may organize believers for cooperative ministry, but it is not the thing itself
A worship service It is one expression of the Church's life, not its definition

Take away the building, cancel the service, dissolve the denomination, and the Church still exists, because the Church is not a place or an event. It is a people.

What follows practically

If the Church is a people rather than a place, belonging is not primarily about attendance. It is about identity and participation. You do not simply go to church the way you might go to a store. You are the Church. Church membership is not a transaction you complete by showing up. It is a life you live in union with Christ and communion with His people.

4. A Royal Priesthood

Peter's description of believers as a royal priesthood deserves careful attention.

Under the old covenant, only a specific tribe, the Levites, and within that tribe only certain families, could serve as priests. Access to God's presence was mediated, restricted, and hedged with regulations. Under the new covenant, every believer has direct access to God through Christ, and every believer is called to serve as a priest, offering spiritual sacrifices of praise, intercession, and service.

There is no class of believers who are closer to God than others. There is no professional clergy that stands between ordinary Christians and the throne of grace. Every member of Christ's Body has been given priestly access and priestly responsibility.
Chapter One

5. The Body: Two Temptations Confronted

If chapter one established who the Church is, chapter two asks how she functions. Paul's favourite metaphor is the human body.

In 1 Corinthians 12 he is writing to a congregation that had confused spiritual gifts with spiritual status, ranking themselves according to which gifts they exercised. Paul confronts two opposite temptations.

The first temptation is self-disqualification. He imagines the foot saying, because I am not a hand, I do not belong to the body. Does that make it any less a part of the body? Every part belongs, regardless of how visible or celebrated its function.

The second temptation is contempt. The eye cannot say to the hand, I do not need you. Paul goes further, insisting that those parts of the body that seem weaker are indispensable, and that the parts we think less honourable we treat with special honour.

This is a remarkable inversion of how the world typically assigns value. The world elevates the visible, the articulate, the powerful. Paul insists that within the Body of Christ, every part is necessary, and the parts that seem weakest deserve the greatest care and honor.
Chapter Two

Paul is describing more than an organizational chart. He is describing a living organism in which every member's health affects the health of the whole. If one part suffers, every part suffers with it.

A believer who withdraws from the Body because they feel their gift is insignificant harms the whole congregation, not just themselves. A believer who refuses to value someone else's contribution because it seems less impressive damages the Body's ability to function.

6. Humility and Function

Romans 12 develops the theme with a different emphasis, focusing on sober self-assessment. Paul urges believers not to think of themselves more highly than they ought, but to think with sober judgment.

What stands out is the connection Paul draws between humility and function. A believer who has an accurate view of their own gifts, neither inflated nor diminished, is free to serve without either arrogance or self-pity. They know what they have been given, and they use it.

7. Why the Gifts Were Given

Ephesians 4 explains the purpose. Christ gave some to be apostles, some prophets, some evangelists, and some pastors and teachers, to equip His people for works of service, so that the body of Christ may be built up.

Gifts are not given for personal display. They are given so that the entire Body might reach unity in the faith and become mature.

The maturing has a specific goal: that we will no longer be infants, tossed back and forth by the waves and blown here and there by every wind of teaching. A congregation full of believers who never learn to discern sound doctrine from false teaching remains perpetually vulnerable to manipulation.

That phrase, speaking the truth in love, captures the balance the Body is meant to embody. A congregation can drift toward one of two errors.

Error What it looks like
Truth without love Correcting and confronting without warmth or patience
Love without truth Tolerating error and sin in the name of acceptance

Paul refuses both extremes. Growth happens when correction is delivered with genuine affection and affection is grounded in unwavering commitment to what is true.

8. Christ as Head

Colossians gives the final and most important piece. Christ is the head of the body, the church.

Every metaphor about the Body ultimately points back to this. The Body does not generate its own direction. It does not decide its own mission. It receives direction from its Head. A body disconnected from its head is not simply weakened, it is dead.

On private devotion as a substitute

Dr. Kay addresses this directly. Private devotion matters enormously, and it was never meant to replace corporate function. You were given a gift, not primarily for your own private enjoyment of God, but for the building up of the Body you belong to. There is no such thing as a solitary limb thriving apart from the body it belongs to. A hand cut off from the arm does not become a stronger, more independent hand. It becomes lifeless tissue.

Finally, unity within the Body does not mean uniformity. Some congregations pursue a false unity that suppresses diversity of gifting, personality, and calling in favour of a single acceptable mould. Paul's vision is the opposite. Unity is preserved precisely through diversity, when every differently gifted member submits to Christ as Head and cooperates with every other member.

Discussion Questions

  1. Work through the three substitutes for the Church in section 3. Which one does your own language most often slip into, and what does that reveal?
  2. The royal priesthood abolishes any mediating class between believers and God. Where does a functional clergy-laity divide still operate in the churches you know?
  3. Paul confronts two opposite temptations with the Body metaphor. Which is yours, self-disqualification or contempt? What does it cost your congregation?
  4. Describe a congregation that has truth without love, and one that has love without truth. What does each produce over ten years?
  5. Dr. Kay says a hand cut off does not become more independent, it becomes lifeless tissue. Where have you seen a believer test that claim, and how did it go?

Reflection

Write down the specific contribution you currently make to the Body you belong to. If you cannot name one, write down the reason honestly. Bring either answer to the next session.

Summary

Ekklesia means a people summoned out, and the calling relocates allegiance rather than merely improving people where they stand. The Church belongs to Christ, who called her His own, and the earliest picture of her in Acts is strikingly ordinary and extraordinary at once. She is not a building, a denomination, or a service, which means belonging is a matter of identity and participation rather than attendance. Every member is a priest with direct access and real responsibility, so no mediating class stands between believers and God. Paul's Body metaphor confronts both self-disqualification and contempt, insisting that the weaker-seeming parts are indispensable and that health is genuinely shared. Gifted leaders exist to equip rather than to perform, aiming at a maturity that can speak the truth in love without collapsing into either coldness or permissiveness. And all of it depends on connection to the Head. Lesson 2 takes up two further images.

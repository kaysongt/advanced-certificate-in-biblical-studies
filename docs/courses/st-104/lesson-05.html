Part Three of the textbook turns from what God has done for us to what God begins to do within us. Having been declared righteous, the believer is not left unchanged.

Three doctrines describe the new life, and they are easily confused because they arrive together at the same moment. Keeping them distinct is the work of this lesson.

DoctrineWhat changes
RegenerationYour nature, from spiritually dead to spiritually alive
AdoptionYour relationship, from stranger to son or daughter
Union with ChristYour identity, joined to Him so completely that His story becomes yours

Key Terms

Regeneration
The Spirit's impartation of a new nature and a new capacity to know, love, and follow God that did not exist before.
Reformation
A person deciding to live differently through determined effort. It operates within the nature already possessed rather than replacing it.
AdoptionGreek huiothesia
The change in relationship from stranger to son or daughter, granting full legal standing as heir.
Spirit of Bondage
Paul's phrase for relating to God out of fear and anxious obligation, which adoption replaces.
Union with Christ
The believer's spiritual joining to Christ, such that His death, resurrection, and life become genuinely theirs.
Joint Heirs
Paul's term in Romans 8:17. Under Roman law an adopted son received full inheritance rights, not a diminished share.

1. Regeneration: A New Nature

Jesus told Nicodemus that unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God. That which is born of the flesh is flesh, and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit.

Human effort, no matter how sincere, cannot produce this new birth, because flesh can only reproduce after its own kind. Spiritual life requires a spiritual origin.

Regeneration is new creation. It is the impartation of a new nature, a new capacity to know, love, and follow God that did not exist within a person before.
Chapter Nine

It is significant that Jesus chose to have this conversation with a man as religious and accomplished as Nicodemus. If anyone could have argued that religious pedigree, careful law-keeping, or a position of respected leadership qualified him for the kingdom, it would have been Nicodemus. Jesus dismantles that assumption immediately.

Paul adds precision in Titus. Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy He saved us, through the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit.

2. Regeneration Is Not Reformation

The two are often confused, and the distinction is worth stating carefully.

Reformation describes a person deciding to live differently, adopting new habits, disciplines, or a moral code through determined effort. It can produce genuine, observable change, and it is not without value. But it operates entirely within the same nature the person already possessed, polishing the surface of a life without altering what lies beneath.

Regeneration is categorically different. It does not merely adjust behaviour. It replaces the underlying spiritual capacity from which behaviour flows.

Reformation asks a person to try harder with the same heart. Regeneration gives them, for the first time, a heart genuinely inclined toward God.
Chapter Nine

This is why the New Testament speaks of believers receiving a new heart and a new spirit, language borrowed from Ezekiel.

Note also what regeneration settles about pride and despair. If regeneration were something we produced through moral striving, every believer would have grounds for either pride at having achieved their own rebirth, or despair at having failed to achieve it thoroughly enough. Because it is entirely the Spirit's work, both lose their footing. You did not birth yourself into God's family through willpower, and you cannot un-birth yourself through failure.

John's first epistle offers practical markers by which the reality of new birth shows itself: increasingly practising righteousness rather than habitual sin, increasingly loving other believers, and increasingly overcoming the pull of the world. None of these describe sinless perfection. They describe a genuine trajectory.

3. Adoption: A New Relationship

Of all the pictures the New Testament uses for the believer's new identity, adoption may be the most tender and, for many people, the most healing.

Paul develops it most fully in Romans 8. As many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God. For you did not receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but you received the Spirit of adoption by whom we cry out, Abba, Father.

The contrast Paul establishes is worth dwelling on.

Spirit of BondageSpirit of Adoption
Relates to God out of fear and obligationRelates out of secure belonging
Anxious sense of needing to earn approvalConfidence rooted in relationship
Always working, never certain the work is enoughCries Abba, Father

Galatians traces the historical path that made this possible, rooting the transformation in a specific historical moment, the incarnation, rather than presenting it as an abstract spiritual principle. Because the Son entered our condition fully, we could be brought fully into His.

Ephesians places adoption within God's eternal purposes, describing believers as predestined to adoption according to the good pleasure of His will.

Adoption was not a reluctant concession God made because He had run out of better options. It was something He delighted to do, planned before the foundation of the world.

4. What Roman Adoption Adds

The ancient practice sheds real light on the theological point. In Roman culture an adopted son was not treated as a second-class member of the family, tolerated but held at arm's length from natural-born children. Adoption under Roman law completely erased the adoptee's old identity and debts, granting full legal status as an heir, equal in every respect to any child born naturally into the family.

This is precisely the picture Paul intends when he calls believers joint heirs with Christ. We do not receive some diminished portion of the inheritance. We receive the full inheritance, alongside Christ Himself.

The fear this dismantles

So many people carry a quiet fear that they are only tolerated rather than truly wanted, that their place in any family or community is conditional and could be revoked if they fail badly enough. Your place in God's family was not earned by good behaviour, and it cannot be forfeited by bad behaviour, because it was never based on your performance in the first place.

Adoption also transforms prayer. A slave approaches a master with requests framed by obligation and uncertainty. A child approaches a loving father confident not because the request is small but because the relationship itself is secure regardless of the outcome.

Paul does not present this identity as one that removes believers from hardship. In the very passage where he describes believers as joint heirs, he adds a sobering qualification: if indeed we suffer with Him, that we may also be glorified together. Adoption gives believers a framework for suffering that neither denies its reality nor allows it the final word.

5. Union with Christ: A New Identity

If I had to identify a single truth that unlocks nearly every other doctrine of the Christian life, I would point to union with Christ.
Chapter Eleven

Paul introduces it with unmistakable clarity in Galatians 2:20. Notice that Paul does not simply say Christ helps him or influences him. He says Christ lives in him, and that his own former identity has been, in some profound spiritual sense, crucified and replaced.

Romans 6 develops the doctrine in its most systematic and practical form. Paul opens by addressing a possible misunderstanding of grace: shall we continue in sin that grace may abound? His answer is emphatic, and his reasoning flows directly from union.

Believers who have been baptized into Christ Jesus have been baptized into His death, buried with Him through baptism into death, so that just as Christ was raised, we also should walk in newness of life.

This is a remarkable claim. Paul is not merely saying Christ's death and resurrection benefit us in some external, transactional way. He is saying that believers were, in a spiritual sense, actually present with Christ in His death and resurrection. Our old self was crucified with Him, that we should no longer be slaves of sin.

This is why Paul can tell believers to reckon themselves dead indeed to sin but alive to God. Not because sin has lost all its pull, but because our fundamental identity has genuinely changed.

6. Baptism as the Visible Picture

Baptism functions in the New Testament as the visible picture of this invisible union. Going down into the water pictures being buried with Christ, and coming up out of the water pictures being raised with Him to walk in newness of life.

It is not the water itself that saves a person, but baptism dramatizes, in a way words alone cannot, the reality that a believer's old identity has genuinely ended and a new one has genuinely begun.

7. Hidden, Raised, and Seated

Colossians describes the practical implications. If then you were raised with Christ, seek those things which are above. For you died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God.

Your truest identity is not fully visible even to yourself right now. It is hidden, safely held within Christ's own relationship with the Father, protected from anything that might otherwise threaten to unravel it.
Chapter Eleven

Ephesians pushes further. God made us alive together with Christ, raised us up together, and made us sit together in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus. Notice the tense. Paul does not describe these as future hopes. He describes them as accomplished facts.

This might sound like poetic exaggeration until you consider how thoroughly it reorients daily living.

  • His victory over sin is genuinely your victory, not merely an example you are trying to imitate through your own strength.
  • His righteousness is genuinely your righteousness, not merely a legal fiction applied to your account.
  • His resurrection life is genuinely at work within you, not merely a future hope.

Jesus offered His disciples an image of this the night before His crucifixion. He described Himself as the vine and His followers as the branches. A branch does not strain or labour to produce fruit through independent effort. It simply remains connected to the vine, and the fruit appears as the natural consequence of that connection.

Union with Christ takes every other doctrine we have studied so far, grace, faith, justification, regeneration, adoption, and binds them together into a single, coherent reality. You are not simply forgiven by Christ. You are joined to Him, so completely that His story has become your story.
Chapter Eleven

Reflection

Write out Galatians 2:20 by hand. Then underline every phrase that describes something already accomplished rather than something you are working toward. Sit with the proportion.

Summary

Three doctrines arrive together at conversion and must be kept distinct. Regeneration changes your nature, imparting a capacity to know and love God that did not previously exist, and it differs from reformation as a new heart differs from a harder-working old one. Because it is wholly the Spirit's work, it removes the footing for both pride and despair. Adoption changes your relationship, replacing the spirit of bondage with the Spirit of adoption, and the Roman practice Paul draws on granted full heir status rather than a diminished share, which dismantles the fear of being merely tolerated. Union with Christ changes your identity, joining you to Him so completely that His death, resurrection, and seated position are genuinely yours, pictured visibly in baptism and described by Paul in the past tense. Union is the truth that binds grace, faith, justification, regeneration, and adoption into one coherent reality. Topic 6 traces where this new life is going.

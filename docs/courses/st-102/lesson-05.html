If you remove every other event from the story of Jesus and leave only the cross, you still have the single most important moment in the history of the world. Every prophecy pointed toward it. Every sacrifice offered under the old covenant anticipated it. Every longing of the human heart for forgiveness finds its answer in it.
Chapter Seven

And yet the cross alone would leave the story unfinished. A crucified Messiah who remained in the tomb would offer, at best, a moving example of self-sacrifice. He would not offer salvation. Paul states the stakes with startling directness: and if Christ is not risen, your faith is futile, you are still in your sins (1 Corinthians 15:17).

These two chapters belong in one lesson because neither event means what it means without the other.

By the end of this topic you will be able to

  1. Explain the meaning of Christ's final word from the cross and what it declares about His work.
  2. Define and distinguish atonement, reconciliation, justification, the great exchange, and victory.
  3. Explain the cry of dereliction and the significance of the torn veil.
  4. Explain the new covenant inaugurated at the Last Supper and its relation to Jeremiah 31.
  5. Present the historical case for the resurrection, including the criteria that distinguish testimony from legend.
  6. State the four things the resurrection confirms, and describe the nature of the resurrected body.

Key Scriptures

So when Jesus had received the sour wine, He said, "It is finished!" And bowing His head, He gave up His spirit.
John 19:30

In the original language this phrase carried the connotation of a debt paid in full, a task completed to its final requirement. Jesus was not merely dying. He was completing a work of cosmic significance, and He wanted everyone within earshot to understand that nothing remained unfinished.

Key Terms

Atonement
The covering and removal of sin through a substitutionary sacrifice.
PropitiationGreek hilasterion
The satisfaction of God's righteous wrath against sin through Christ's sacrifice.
Reconciliation
The restoration of relationship between God and the sinner, turning enemies of God into children of God.
Justification
The legal declaration that a believer stands righteous before God. An immediate, complete verdict rather than a gradual process.
The Great Exchange
Christ taking our sin and crediting His righteousness to everyone who trusts Him (2 Corinthians 5:21).
Cry of Dereliction
Christ's cry from the cross quoting Psalm 22, expressing a real if temporary sense of separation from the Father while bearing our sin.
Firstfruits
The first portion of a harvest, guaranteeing the rest will follow. Paul applies it to Christ's resurrection in 1 Corinthians 15:20.
New Covenant
The covenant promised in Jeremiah 31, sealed in Christ's blood, written on hearts rather than on tablets of stone.

1. Foretold Centuries in Advance

Isaiah, writing roughly seven centuries before the crucifixion, described a suffering servant with such precision that the chapter reads almost like an eyewitness account written after the fact. He was wounded for our transgressions, He was bruised for our iniquities, the chastisement for our peace was upon Him, and by His stripes we are healed (Isaiah 53:5). Isaiah goes on to describe this servant as one led as a lamb to the slaughter, who bore the sin of many, and who made intercession for transgressors (Isaiah 53:7, 12).

No other explanation accounts for a prophecy this specific except that God, who exists outside of time, revealed to Isaiah exactly what His Son would accomplish centuries later.

2. What the Cross Accomplished

The cross accomplished more than any single word can capture. Paul unpacks it from several angles across his letters, and a student should be able to define each.

Atonement. God set forth Christ as a propitiation by His blood, through faith, to demonstrate His righteousness (Romans 3:25). Propitiation means God's righteous wrath against sin was fully satisfied through Christ's sacrifice. Sin against an infinitely holy God demands a response, and at the cross that response fell on Christ instead of on us.

Reconciliation. God demonstrates His own love toward us, in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us (Romans 5:8), and we were reconciled to God through the death of His Son (Romans 5:10). The cross does not merely erase a debt. It restores a broken relationship, turning enemies of God into children of God.

Justification. Believers are justified freely by His grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus (Romans 3:24). This is not a gradual process of becoming acceptable to God through effort. It is an immediate, complete legal verdict, declared over everyone who trusts in what Christ accomplished.

The great exchange. For He made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in Him (2 Corinthians 5:21). Christ, who never sinned, took on our sin. In exchange, everyone who trusts Him receives His righteousness, credited to their account as though they had lived His perfect life themselves.

There is no more astonishing transaction in the entire Bible.
Chapter Seven

Victory. Christ entered the Most Holy Place once for all, having obtained eternal redemption (Hebrews 9:12), a redemption so complete that it never needs to be repeated.

3. The Cry That Reveals the Cost

Among the seven statements Jesus made from the cross, one stands apart in its rawness. Quoting the opening line of Psalm 22, Jesus cried out in a loud voice, My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me (Matthew 27:46).

This was not a cry of doubt or confusion about His mission. It was the cry of the sinless Son, in the moment He bore the full weight of humanity's sin, experiencing something He had never experienced in all of eternity, a real, if temporary, sense of separation from the Father with whom He had enjoyed unbroken fellowship since before the world began.

The cry of dereliction is the sound of that great exchange taking place, the Son absorbing what our sin deserved so that we would never have to hear those words ourselves.
Chapter Seven

4. The Torn Veil

At the precise moment of Christ's death, Matthew records the veil of the temple, the heavy curtain separating the Most Holy Place from the rest of the sanctuary, torn in two from top to bottom (Matthew 27:51).

The direction of the tear matters. A veil torn from the bottom might suggest human effort finally breaking through a barrier from the outside. A veil torn from the top signals that God Himself removed the barrier from His side.

Hebrews later develops this at length, describing a new and living way into the Most Holy Place, opened for us through the veil, that is, through His flesh (Hebrews 10:19 to 20).

5. A New Covenant Sealed in Blood

On the night before His crucifixion, Jesus took bread and gave it to His disciples, saying, this is My body which is given for you, do this in remembrance of Me (Luke 22:19). Then He took the cup, saying, this cup is the new covenant in My blood, which is shed for you (Luke 22:20).

Every faithful Jew at that table would have understood the weight of what He was declaring. Jeremiah had spoken centuries earlier of a coming day when God would make a new covenant with His people, different from the covenant made at Sinai, one in which God would write His law on their hearts and remember their sins no more (Jeremiah 31:31 to 34).

Why covenant and cross cannot be separated

The cross was not merely an isolated act of suffering and sacrifice. It was the ratification of an entirely new relationship between God and His people, built not on external law-keeping but on internal transformation, not on the blood of animals repeated endlessly but on the single, sufficient blood of the Son of God. Every time believers gather at the Lord's Table they are proclaiming, generation after generation, the covenant that Christ's death established.

6. Why the Cross Cannot Be Bypassed

Some voices, inside and outside the church, have tried to present a version of Christianity that minimizes the cross, offering Jesus merely as a moral example or a source of general inspiration. Strip away the cross and you strip away the gospel itself.

There is no alternative path

This is not one option among many for dealing with sin. It is the only sacrifice God Himself has declared sufficient, and it is finished, complete, requiring nothing added to it from us except the empty hands of faith that receive what Christ has already accomplished.

The cross does not minimize the seriousness of sin. If anything it reveals how seriously God takes sin, since nothing less than the death of His own Son could satisfy its demands. It also reveals the depth of His mercy, since He was willing to pay that price Himself rather than demand it from us.

7. What the Gospels Record About the Third Day

Each of the four Gospels records the discovery of the empty tomb, and though the accounts differ slightly in the details they choose to emphasize, all four converge on the same central fact. The tomb was found empty on the first day of the week, and Jesus Himself began appearing, alive.

Matthew records the women arriving and being met by an angel who told them, He is not here, for He is risen, as He said (Matthew 28:6). John records Mary Magdalene encountering the risen Jesus in the garden, mistaking Him at first for the gardener before He spoke her name (John 20:11 to 16). Jesus appeared to His disciples in a locked room, inviting Thomas to touch His hands and side (John 20:24 to 27).

Paul, writing roughly two decades after the events, lists a series of appearances including one to more than five hundred believers at once, most of whom, he notes, were still alive at the time of his writing and could be consulted directly (1 Corinthians 15:5 to 6).

8. History, Not Legend

Notice how carefully the New Testament writers ground the resurrection in verifiable detail rather than vague spiritual metaphor. Paul is not writing devotional poetry in 1 Corinthians 15. He is presenting what amounts to a legal case, listing specific witnesses by name.

This kind of specificity is not the language of myth making. It is the language of a man inviting his readers to go and check the facts for themselves.
Chapter Eight

Two further considerations carry weight.

The transformation of the disciples. These were men who scattered in fear at Christ's arrest, and Peter denied even knowing Him three times in a single night. Within weeks the same group was proclaiming the resurrection publicly in Jerusalem, the very city where Jesus had been executed, at enormous personal risk, and most would eventually die for that testimony. People do not typically face torture and execution for a story they know to be false.

The first witnesses were women. In that time and culture a woman's testimony carried little legal or social weight. If the accounts had been invented to persuade a sceptical audience, this is precisely the detail a fabricator would have changed. That the Gospel writers preserved it despite the cultural cost suggests they were recording what actually happened.

9. What the Resurrection Confirms

It confirms His deity. Jesus was declared to be the Son of God with power according to the Spirit of holiness, by the resurrection from the dead (Romans 1:4). Anyone can claim divine authority. Only God can back that claim by walking out of a sealed and guarded tomb.

It secures the believer's justification. He was delivered up because of our offences, and was raised because of our justification (Romans 4:25). The cross dealt with the penalty of sin. The resurrection demonstrates that the penalty has been fully and finally paid, since death, the wages of sin, could not hold Him.

It guarantees our future resurrection. Paul describes Christ as the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep (1 Corinthians 15:20). The firstfruits were the first portion of a harvest, guaranteeing the rest would follow. Christ's resurrection is not an isolated miracle performed for His benefit alone.

It establishes the foundation of Christian hope. Peter praises God who according to His abundant mercy has begotten us again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead (1 Peter 1:3). Notice the phrase living hope. This is not wishful thinking. It is hope grounded in a historical event.

10. A Body Real, Yet Transformed

The nature of Christ's resurrected body teaches us something about our own future. The risen Jesus was not a ghost. He invited Thomas to touch His hands and side (John 20:27). He ate broiled fish in front of His disciples to demonstrate He was not a spirit (Luke 24:39 to 43). His body bore the very scars of His crucifixion, evidence that the body that rose was the same body that had died.

And yet the same body displayed capacities beyond ordinary human experience, appearing in a locked room without need of an opened door (John 20:19), and being unrecognizable at times until He chose to reveal Himself (Luke 24:15 to 16, 30 to 31).

This combination of continuity and transformation matters enormously. Paul explains that what is sown in corruption is raised in incorruption, what is sown in dishonour is raised in glory, what is sown in weakness is raised in power (1 Corinthians 15:42 to 44).

Your future resurrection body will be genuinely yours, continuous with the body you have now, and yet gloriously transformed, freed from every limitation of weakness, sickness, and decay.
Chapter Eight

Paul draws the whole argument to its conclusion with a taunt aimed at death itself: O Death, where is your sting? O Hades, where is your victory? (1 Corinthians 15:55).

Reflection

Read Isaiah 53 slowly, then read Matthew 27. Note three specific points where the prophecy and the record correspond. Sit with the fact that seven centuries separate them.

Summary

The cross was foretold in Isaiah 53 with a precision no ordinary foresight explains, and Christ's final word declared a debt paid in full. It accomplished atonement, reconciliation, justification, the great exchange, and victory, five angles on one finished work. The cry of dereliction is the sound of that exchange taking place, and the veil torn from the top announces that God removed the barrier from His own side. At the table the night before, Christ inaugurated the covenant Jeremiah had promised. Yet the cross alone would leave the story unfinished. The tomb was found empty, the appearances were public and verifiable, cowardly disciples became witnesses who died for their testimony, and the accounts preserve details no fabricator would have invented. The resurrection confirms His deity, secures our justification, guarantees our own resurrection, and grounds a living hope. Topic 6 follows Him upward.

Part Two of the textbook turns from the Person of Christ to the work of Christ, and it begins with a structure drawn straight out of Israel's history.

In the Old Testament, God appointed three distinct offices to lead and shepherd His people. Prophets spoke God's word to the people. Priests represented the people before God, offering sacrifices on their behalf. Kings governed the people under God's authority.

No single individual in the Old Testament ever held all three offices at once. The roles were kept deliberately separate, each pointing forward to something greater still to come. In Jesus Christ, all three converge in a single Person, and in Him each office reaches its perfect and final fulfillment.

By the end of this topic you will be able to

  1. Explain the origin and purpose of the three Old Testament offices and how each is fulfilled in Christ.
  2. Show what would be missing from our salvation if Christ held only one or two of the three.
  3. Explain the meaning of the title Christ and what confessing it commits you to.
  4. Describe how the three offices continue to shape daily discipleship.
  5. Explain the announcement of the Kingdom at Nazareth and the nature of the authority the crowds recognized in His teaching.
  6. Explain the purpose of the miracles as signs, and the significance of the boundaries His ministry crossed.

1. Prophet: The One Who Perfectly Reveals God

Moses told Israel that God would raise up a Prophet like himself, and that the people were to listen to Him (Deuteronomy 18:15). For centuries Israel waited to see who this promised Prophet would be.

Jesus fulfilled the expectation completely, and He did so in a way no earlier prophet had. The prophets before Him received God's word and then delivered it to the people. Jesus did not merely deliver a message from God.

He was, in His own Person, the message. The prophets spoke about God. The Son speaks as God, and reveals God, because He is God.
Chapter Five

Hebrews frames the contrast exactly: God, who at various times and in different ways spoke in time past to the fathers by the prophets, has in these last days spoken to us by His Son (Hebrews 1:1 to 2).

His teaching carried an authority the crowds immediately noticed. He did not simply interpret the law. He spoke with the very authority that gave the law in the first place.

2. Priest: The One Who Offers and Intercedes

The Old Testament priesthood, established through Aaron, offered sacrifices for sin year after year, a system Hebrews describes as ultimately powerless to take away sin permanently, since it required constant repetition (Hebrews 10:1 to 4).

Christ's priesthood operates on an entirely different order, the order of Melchizedek, a priesthood based not on genealogy but on the power of an indestructible life (Hebrews 7:16).

As our Great High Priest, Jesus did something no Levitical priest could do. He offered Himself as the sacrifice. He was both the priest performing the offering and the offering itself. Unlike high priests who needed to offer sacrifices daily, first for their own sins and then for the people's, Christ did this once for all when He offered up Himself (Hebrews 7:27).

His priestly work did not end at the cross. He continues even now to intercede for His people, since He always lives to make intercession for them (Hebrews 7:25). We return to this in Topic 6.

3. King: The One Who Reigns With All Authority

The Davidic covenant promised that a King from David's line would establish an everlasting throne (2 Samuel 7:12 to 13). The Psalms anticipate this King, describing the Lord's declaration to His Anointed: You are My Son, today I have begotten You. Ask of Me, and I will give You the nations for Your inheritance (Psalm 2:7 to 8). Psalm 110 goes further, picturing this King seated at God's right hand until His enemies are made His footstool, and describing Him simultaneously as a priest forever, a merger of offices fulfilled in Christ alone.

Jesus is King now, though His kingdom does not yet appear in its final, visible fullness. He reigns over His church, over the hearts of those who submit to Him, and over the unfolding of history itself, even when the world does not recognize His authority. Revelation pulls back the curtain on His future visible reign, describing Him coming on a white horse with a name written on His robe and thigh: KING OF KINGS AND LORD OF LORDS (Revelation 19:16).

Chapter Five

5. The Title We Use Without Thinking

Christ is not a surname. It is a title, the Greek equivalent of the Hebrew Messiah, meaning the Anointed One. In the Old Testament, anointing with oil marked the formal installation of a prophet, priest, or king into office.

When the New Testament calls Jesus the Christ, it is making a comprehensive claim: that He is the one anointed to fulfill all three offices at once, not by the pouring of oil as with His predecessors, but by the anointing of the Holy Spirit Himself, who descended upon Him at His baptism and remained (John 1:32 to 33).

What you are saying

Every time you say the name Jesus Christ, you are making a theological confession, whether you realize it or not, declaring Him to be the long-promised Anointed One who fulfills the entire pattern established across the Old Testament.

6. The Three Offices in Daily Discipleship

These are not merely historical categories.

As Prophet, Christ still speaks to His people, primarily through the Scriptures He inspired and through the illumination of His Spirit. When you open your Bible seeking direction, you are coming under the ongoing prophetic ministry of Christ.

As Priest, Christ continues to mediate your relationship with God, both in the finished sense of the sacrifice offered once and in the ongoing sense of the intercession He performs now. Every prayer you offer travels to the Father through your Great High Priest. You never approach God on your own merit.

As King, Christ exercises authority over your life, your circumstances, and your obedience. To call Him King is not merely to acknowledge cosmic sovereignty over galaxies and nations. It is to submit your own choices, your own schedule, your own resources to His rightful rule.

An incomplete faith

A faith that celebrates Christ as Prophet and Priest while ignoring His kingship is an incomplete faith, one that wants His revelation and His forgiveness while withholding genuine obedience.

Each office found partial, imperfect expression in Israel before converging in Christ. Moses stood as a prophet, yet a flawed one, barred from the promised land for his own disobedience. Aaron and his descendants served as priests, yet men who needed atonement for their own sin before they could offer sacrifices for others. David and his royal descendants sat on the throne, yet kings whose reigns were marked, almost without exception, by moral failure. Every one of them pointed beyond himself. In Jesus, the shadow gave way to the substance.

7. The Kingdom Announced

When Jesus began His public ministry, He did not open with abstract theology. He opened with an announcement.

Returning to His hometown synagogue in Nazareth, He read from Isaiah, describing one who would preach good news to the poor, proclaim liberty to captives, give sight to the blind, and announce the acceptable year of the Lord (Isaiah 61:1 to 2, quoted in Luke 4:16 to 19). Then He rolled up the scroll, sat down, and said the words that must have stunned everyone in the room: today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing (Luke 4:21).

Matthew summarizes the shape of the ministry that followed with a phrase he repeats: teaching in their synagogues, preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing all kinds of sickness and all kinds of disease among the people (Matthew 4:23).

His teaching carried an authority the crowds recognized instinctively, noting that He taught as one having authority, and not as the scribes (Matthew 7:29). The scribes quoted earlier authorities to validate their teaching. Jesus needed no such validation. He spoke as the source of the truth He was proclaiming.

His parables deserve mention here. Rather than lecturing with dense theological arguments, Jesus told stories rooted in ordinary life: a sower scattering seed, a woman searching for a lost coin, a father running to embrace a wayward son. These carried the weight of Kingdom truth precisely because they were so accessible, inviting listeners to see their own lives reflected back at them, often in ways that convicted before they comforted.

8. Miracles as Signs

Every miracle served a purpose beyond simple compassion, though compassion was always present. John describes the miracles as signs, deliberately choosing a word that points beyond the event to what it reveals. He states his purpose explicitly: these are written that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life in His name (John 20:31).

Peter summarized the pattern when preaching to Cornelius, describing how God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with Him (Acts 10:38).

9. A Ministry That Crossed Every Boundary

One of the most striking features of His earthly ministry is how consistently He crossed social and religious boundaries His culture treated as fixed.

He spoke openly with a Samaritan woman at a well, a conversation that would have scandalized His disciples on multiple levels at once (John 4:7 to 26). He touched lepers whom the law required to be quarantined, and rather than becoming ceremonially unclean Himself, His touch brought cleansing (Mark 1:40 to 42). He allowed a woman with a reputation for sin to wash His feet with her tears in the home of a scandalized Pharisee, and rather than rebuking her, He commended her faith and pronounced her sins forgiven (Luke 7:36 to 50).

Alongside this compassion ran sharp confrontation toward religious pride. His harshest words are reserved not for the openly sinful but for those who used religious position to burden others while avoiding genuine transformation themselves.

It is not a kingdom built on outward religious performance. It is a kingdom built on genuine transformation of the heart, available to anyone humble enough to receive it and closed to anyone too proud to admit their need for it.
Chapter Six

10. Discipleship, the Sermon, and the Undercurrent

Jesus did not simply broadcast a message to crowds and move on. He gathered a small band of disciples and poured His life into them over three years, teaching not only through formal instruction but through shared life, shared meals, and shared ministry. The Kingdom He announced was never meant to be received passively.

The Sermon on the Mount gives the clearest sustained picture of what He meant. Blessing rests not on the powerful but on the poor in spirit, not on the self-assured but on those who mourn, not on the aggressive but on the meek, not on the satisfied but on those who hunger and thirst for righteousness (Matthew 5:3 to 6). He calls His followers past external compliance, addressing not only murder but the anger preceding it, not only adultery but the lust preceding it (Matthew 5:21 to 28). It reorders priorities away from anxious accumulation and toward seeking God's Kingdom and His righteousness first (Matthew 6:33).

Underneath every sermon, every miracle, and every act of compassion ran a single undercurrent: perfect obedience to the Father's will. Jesus told His disciples that His very food was to do the will of the One who sent Him and to finish His work (John 4:34).

His earthly ministry was never a random collection of good deeds and wise sayings. It was a coherent, deliberate unveiling of God's Kingdom, confirmed by fulfilled prophecy, validated by supernatural power, and grounded in perfect obedience, all of it pointing toward what would follow in Jerusalem.

Summary

Israel's three offices of prophet, priest, and king were kept deliberately separate, each held by flawed men who pointed beyond themselves. In Christ all three converge and reach their fulfillment. He is the Prophet who does not merely deliver the message but is the message, the Priest who offered Himself once for all and still intercedes, and the King whose reign is real now and will be undeniable later. Remove any one and something essential to our salvation is missing. The very title Christ confesses all three at once. And His earthly ministry was the deliberate unveiling of the Kingdom He announced at Nazareth: teaching that carried its own authority, parables that convicted before they comforted, miracles that functioned as signs pointing to His identity, and a consistent crossing of boundaries that showed whose Kingdom this was. Underneath all of it ran perfect obedience to the Father, carrying Him toward Jerusalem. Topic 5 goes there.
